How to make ambient music in your browser

By Tongkit Leong · September 2, 2026 · 8 min read

Ambient music is built from a slow drone bed, a reverb-drenched pad that never quite resolves, sparse melodic fragments, and long, gentle automation swells instead of a beat — and you can layer all four from scratch in the Tracks browser studio with no software to install and no account to create.

What makes a track feel ambient

Ambient production works almost opposite to most of the genres people ask about — there's usually no kick drum driving the track, no verse-chorus arc, and no hook to hum back. What replaces all that is texture and patience. A handful of choices consistently produce that feeling:

  • A sustained low drone — one note, or a very slow two-note interval, held for the length of the whole track as a foundation everything else floats over.
  • Reverb-soaked pads — chords with a slow attack and a very long release, sent hard to reverb so individual notes dissolve into a continuous wash instead of sounding like discrete hits.
  • Sparse, isolated fragments — a few notes, a plucked SoundFont sound, or a short recorded phrase, spaced out by bars of silence so each one feels like it's drifting through the space rather than driving it.
  • Slow automation instead of rhythm — filter cutoffs, volumes, and reverb sends that move gradually over 16, 32, or even 64 bars, giving the track motion without a pulse.
  • Structure by density, not by section — the track builds and fades by adding or removing layers, not by switching to a different chord progression or beat.

Tracks covers all of this with tools already built in — the subtractive and FM synth for the drone and pad, the 16-pad sampler or SoundFont playback for texture, reverb and delay sends for the wash, and automation lanes on every parameter for the slow movement.

Step 1: Set a slow tempo, or skip the grid entirely

Open the Tracks studio and set the BPM low — 50 to 75 is a common range. Ambient rarely has a beat you actually feel, so the tempo mostly exists to keep your note lengths and automation lanes readable on the timeline, not to set a groove. If you're building something that's entirely drone and pad with no rhythmic element at all, the exact number matters even less; pick something low and move on.

Step 2: Build a drone bed

Add a synth track and open the subtractive synth. Choose a sine or soft triangle wave — you want something with almost no edge to it — and set a low-pass filter with the cutoff turned well down so the tone stays round and distant rather than bright.

In the piano roll, hold a single low note for the length of the entire track, or write a very slow alternation between two notes a fifth or an octave apart, each held for many bars. This drone is the floor everything else sits on, so it should be felt more than noticed — keep its volume well under the pad and texture layers.

Step 3: Layer an evolving pad chord

On a second synth track, voice a slow, open chord — spread the notes wide rather than stacking them tightly, and lean toward suspended or open-fifth voicings that don't resolve strongly in either direction. Set a long attack and a long release on the amplitude envelope so each chord fades in and out rather than starting and stopping cleanly.

Send the pad heavily to a reverb bus with a long decay time. This is the single most important move in ambient production: enough reverb turns a handful of chord changes into a continuous, shifting wash where individual notes are hard to pick out. See Reverb and delay explained for how the sends work in Tracks, and don't be shy about pushing the send further than you would on a pop or electronic mix.

Adding a second texture layer

A soft string, choir, or pad SoundFont layered quietly underneath or on top of the synth pad adds a second, slightly different texture without adding a second idea — it's still the same slow chord, just with more grain to it. Keep it low in the mix so it thickens the pad rather than competing with it.

Step 4: Add sparse melodic texture

On a third track, place a handful of isolated notes — a soft mallet or bell SoundFont, or a patch from the 16-pad sampler — spaced several bars apart rather than in a repeating pattern. Each one should feel like a fragment that drifted into the mix and out again, not a melody you could hum back.

Send this layer to both reverb and delay so each note trails off into a cloud of repeats rather than stopping abruptly. If you have a recording of a real instrument, a field recording, or a spoken phrase, this is also a good place for it — record it in with the studio's mic input, then time-stretch or repitch it to sit inside the track's key and mood. See Time-stretch and repitch in the browser for how that works.

Step 5: Automate slow movement

Static layers get boring fast, even in a genre built on stillness. Open the automation lane on the pad track and draw the filter cutoff rising and falling gradually over 16 or 32 bars, so the tone opens up and closes back down like something breathing. Do the same with a reverb send level, or with the drone's volume, so no single parameter sits completely flat for the whole track.

The key difference from automation in a beat-driven genre is timescale — instead of a swell over one phrase, think in terms of the whole track. See Automation in your mix for the general mechanics of drawing and editing automation curves in Tracks.

Step 6: Arrange without a verse-chorus structure

Ambient tracks rarely use pop song structure. A shape that works well instead:

  1. Build (30–60 seconds): the drone enters alone, then the pad fades in underneath it.
  2. Sustain (2–4 minutes): the full layer stack — drone, pad, and sparse texture — sits together, shifting slowly through automation rather than through new material.
  3. Thin (30–60 seconds): drop the texture layer, leaving drone and pad, or drop the pad and leave the drone alone with the texture.
  4. Fade: let the reverb tails ring out and the remaining layers fade to silence rather than cutting the track off.

Block these sections out on the Tracks timeline as long, simple regions — see How to arrange a song if you want the general approach to structuring a track before you specialize it for ambient's slower pacing.

Step 7: Mix and export

Ambient mixing is less about separation and more about not letting the wash collapse into noise:

  1. Keep the drone quiet enough that it's felt as low-end weight, not heard as a distinct note.
  2. Watch your reverb sends collectively — three or four layers all sent hard to long reverb can build up into a wall of sound that loses definition. Pull one or two back if the mix feels smeared rather than spacious.
  3. Use a gentle EQ cut on the low end of the pad and texture tracks so they don't compete with the drone for the same space.
  4. Skip heavy compression — ambient generally wants a wide, breathing dynamic range rather than a squashed, consistent level.

When it feels right at a comfortable volume, export to WAV for the cleanest file or MP3 for sharing — see How to export your song to MP3 or WAV for the full steps. Your project stays in your browser the whole time; Tracks never uploads it anywhere.

Frequently asked questions

Does ambient music need a beat at all?

No. A lot of ambient music has no drums or rhythmic element whatsoever — just drone, pad, and texture. If you do want a beat, keep it minimal and distant: a soft kick or a filtered noise hit spaced far apart, sent to reverb like everything else, rather than a driving pattern.

What BPM should I use if there's no beat?

It barely matters. Pick something low, 50 to 75 BPM, mainly so your automation lanes and note lengths line up cleanly on the Tracks timeline. The tempo isn't something a listener will feel the way they would in a dance track.

How long should an ambient track be?

Longer than most genres — 4 to 8 minutes is common, and some pieces run much longer, because the genre relies on patience and slow change rather than a compact hook. Start with something in the 4-to-6-minute range while you're learning the pacing.

Can I use field recordings or my own voice in an ambient track?

Yes. Record any source through the studio's mic input, then use time-stretch, repitch, reverb, and delay to fold it into the texture. A recorded phrase pitched down and drenched in reverb is a classic ambient move.

Why does everything sound muddy when I add more layers?

It's almost always the reverb sends stacking up. Each layer sounds fine sent to reverb on its own, but three or four sent hard to the same long-decay bus can blur into an indistinct wash. Pull back the send on whichever layer matters least at that moment, or automate sends to drop out during the busiest sections.
