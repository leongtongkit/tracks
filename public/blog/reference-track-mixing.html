How to use a reference track when mixing

By Tongkit Leong · September 26, 2026 · 8 min read

A reference track is a finished, professionally mixed song in your genre that you play back-to-back against your own mix to check level, tone, and width. Your ears adapt to whatever they've been hearing, so a reference gives you a fixed outside point to compare against instead of trusting a sense of "loud" or "bright" that drifts the longer you listen.

Why your ears need a reference

Ear fatigue is real and it happens fast. Spend twenty minutes staring at a vocal fader and your ears quietly recalibrate to whatever level and tone you've been hearing — a mix that felt too bright at the start can start to feel normal by the end of the session, and a bass that felt thin can start to feel fine. This is why a mix that sounded finished at 1am often sounds thin, harsh, or oddly balanced the next morning with fresh ears.

A reference track breaks that drift. Instead of relying on a memory of how loud or bright things "should" be, you play a commercial mix you already know sounds good, then flip back to your own track at the same perceived volume. The gap between the two tells you something concrete: your low end is quieter than theirs, your vocal sits further back, your high end is harsher. That's specific, actionable information — a lot more useful than a vague feeling that something's off.

Picking a good reference track

Not every song you like makes a good reference. Look for:

  • Same genre and similar arrangement. A dense, layered pop mix and a sparse acoustic ballad have completely different mix goals — comparing your trap beat against a folk song tells you nothing useful. Pick something with a similar instrument count and energy to your own track.
  • A mix you already know well. You want something you've heard enough times that you have an instinct for how it should sound, not something you're hearing critically for the first time.
  • A clean, well-known master, not a random upload. Streaming platforms and re-encoded YouTube rips can be re-compressed or loudness-processed in ways that color the tone. Where possible, use a source file you trust.
  • Two or three references, not one. A single reference can lead you to chase one engineer's specific choices. Two or three tracks in the same lane show you what's actually consistent across the genre versus what's just one mix's personality.

Setting up a fair comparison in Tracks

The single biggest mistake in reference mixing is comparing at different volumes. A louder track always sounds better — bigger, punchier, more exciting — even if the mix underneath is worse. That's a loudness illusion, not a mix quality difference, and it will send you chasing the wrong fix every time.

To compare fairly in Tracks:

  1. Import the reference track onto its own audio track, muted by default so it doesn't bleed into your export.
  2. Set that track's fader so the reference plays back at roughly the same perceived loudness as your own mix — use your ears, or match peak levels on the track meter as a rough starting point.
  3. Solo your mix, listen for a few bars, then solo the reference and listen to the same section. Flip back and forth in short bursts — a few seconds each way — rather than long stretches, since long stretches let your ears re-adapt to whichever one is playing.
  4. Listen for one thing at a time: first overall balance, then just the low end, then just the vocal or lead, then the top end. Trying to judge everything at once is how you end up making five changes that fight each other.

Because this comparison track never gets exported, it's safe to leave it in the project — mute it before you bounce. See how to export your song to MP3 or WAV for the export step once your mix is ready.

What to actually listen for

Tonal balance

Does the reference have more or less low end than yours at the same volume? More top-end sparkle? A voice that sits further forward or further back in the mix? Use the per-track EQ in the Tracks mixer to nudge your own track toward the balance you're hearing — see the EQ cheat sheet by instrument for where each instrument typically needs room.

Width and depth

Commercial mixes are often wider than a rough mix — background elements panned out, a stereo widener or double-tracked part filling the sides, while the kick, bass, and lead vocal stay centered. See panning and stereo width explained for how to build that spread without losing the mix in mono.

Dynamics and punch

Does the reference's drum hit harder, snap faster, sound more controlled? That's usually compression and gain staging, not volume — see compressor vs. limiter explained and gain staging: how to avoid clipping.

Loudness, last

Only compare final loudness once your tonal balance and dynamics already feel close. Chasing loudness first just hides problems under volume instead of fixing them — see how to master a track for streaming for the loudness workflow once the mix itself is right.

The trap: copying instead of comparing

A reference track is there to calibrate your ears, not to hand you a recipe. If you catch yourself trying to match every frequency and every dB of loudness exactly, you've stopped mixing your song and started making a lesser copy of someone else's — and it will usually sound worse, because their mix was built around their specific recordings and arrangement, not yours.

Use the reference to answer specific questions — is my bass too quiet, is my vocal too dull, is my mix too narrow — and then solve each one with your own ears on your own material. The goal is a mix that sits in the same ballpark as professional records in your genre, not a clone of one particular song.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing at different volumes. This is the single most common error and it invalidates almost everything you hear. Match perceived loudness before you judge anything else.
  • Using only one reference. One track teaches you that engineer's taste, not the genre's baseline. Use two or three.
  • Referencing a genre your song isn't in. A dense EDM master and a stripped-back singer-songwriter recording need completely different mix decisions — pick a reference that actually matches your arrangement.
  • Long, uninterrupted listening instead of quick flips. Your ears re-adapt within seconds of switching. Short, frequent A/B flips beat long stretches of either track.
  • Chasing the reference's loudness before fixing tone and balance. A louder version of an unbalanced mix is still an unbalanced mix — just harder to fix once it's squashed.

Frequently asked questions

Won't listening to a reference make my mix sound like a copy?

Not if you use it correctly. A reference calibrates what "balanced" sounds like for your ears in that moment — it answers specific questions about level, tone, and width. Applying every one of its exact settings to your own different recordings is what causes a mix to feel like an imitation, and that's a choice, not something a reference forces on you.

Can I use a reference track without importing it into my project?

You can play it in a separate tab or app, but matching perceived volume by ear across two different players is much harder than doing it inside the same project on its own muted track, where you can flip between the two with one click and know the levels are directly comparable.

How many reference tracks should I use?

Two or three in the same genre and a similar arrangement to your own song works well. One risks copying a single engineer's specific choices; more than three or four starts to blur into contradictory advice.

Should I reference against streaming versions or the original master?

Streaming platforms apply their own loudness normalization on playback, which can make a mix sound different from its original master. If you're only judging tone and balance rather than final loudness, streaming versions are fine — just don't use them to judge exact loudness targets.
