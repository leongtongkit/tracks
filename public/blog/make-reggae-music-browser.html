How to make reggae music in your browser

By Tongkit Leong · September 3, 2026 · 8 min read

Reggae is built from the one-drop drum pattern, an off-beat guitar or organ skank, and a deep, melodic bassline that carries the song more than the drums do — and you can put all three together from scratch in the Tracks browser studio in an afternoon, with no software to install and no account to create.

The anatomy of a reggae track

Reggae came out of Jamaica in the late 1960s, and its identity is almost entirely rhythmic: where the kick lands, where the guitar chops, and how the bass moves underneath it all. A handful of elements show up in nearly every reggae track:

  • The one-drop drum pattern — the kick and snare land together on beat three of the bar, leaving beat one open, which is the opposite of almost every other style of popular music.
  • The off-beat skank — a guitar or organ chord struck sharply on the off-beats (the "and" between the main beats), giving the track its choppy, upstroke feel.
  • A deep, melodic bassline — reggae bass is a lead instrument in its own right, walking between chord tones with its own phrasing instead of just anchoring the root.
  • Horns and vocal harmonies — short horn stabs or close three-part vocal harmony lines that punctuate the chorus and hook sections.
  • Space — reggae arrangements leave real gaps between hits; the groove comes from what's left out as much as what's played.

Tracks covers this end to end — the drum machine for the one-drop pattern, the piano roll for the skank and bassline, and the subtractive synth or SoundFont playback for organ and horn sounds.

Step 1: Set your tempo

Open the Tracks studio and set the BPM in the transport bar. Reggae sits in a relaxed, mid-tempo range:

  • Classic roots reggae: 60–75 BPM. Slow and heavy, with lots of space between hits.
  • Modern and dancehall-leaning reggae: 75–90 BPM. A little more forward motion without losing the groove.

70 BPM is a safe default — slow enough for the one-drop to breathe, fast enough that the track doesn't drag.

Step 2: Program the one-drop drum pattern

Add a track and open the Tracks drum machine. The one-drop is the single most identifiable thing about reggae, and it works by omission: instead of placing the kick on beat one like most genres, you leave beat one empty and land the kick and snare together, hard, on beat three.

A basic bar looks like this: nothing on beat one, a closed hi-hat or rim click ticking through beats one and two, then kick and snare hitting together on beat three, followed by another quiet beat four. Loop that four-bar pattern and resist the urge to fill in beat one — the empty space is what makes the drop on beat three land. See Quantize and swing explained for more on shaping a pattern's feel deliberately.

Keep the snare open and roomy

Reggae snares are usually tuned looser and given more natural decay than a tight pop or trap snare — a touch of reverb helps it feel like it's hitting in a real room. Layer a rimshot or cross-stick sound on some hits for variation, especially in the quieter bars between drops.

Step 3: Add the off-beat skank

Open the piano roll on a new track and voice a simple two- or three-note chord using a clean electric guitar or organ patch (a SoundFont works well here — see SoundFont instruments explained). Instead of playing on the beat, place short, staccato chord stabs only on the off-beats — the gap between beat one and beat two, and between beat two and beat three, and so on.

Keep each stab short: trim the note length down so the chord cuts off almost immediately after it strikes, which is what gives the skank its dry, percussive character rather than a sustained strum. Doubling the skank on both a guitar-style patch and an organ patch, panned slightly apart, thickens it without cluttering the mix.

Step 4: Build a deep, melodic bassline

Open the subtractive synth and program a bassline in the piano roll. Reggae bass is melodic first and rhythmic second: instead of hammering a single root note, walk between the root, third, and fifth of the chord, and let the bass carry as much of the song's melody as the vocal does. See How to write a bassline that locks with the kick drum for how to keep the low end tight against the one-drop pattern specifically.

Use a rounded, low-pass-filtered tone with a slightly slow attack so notes swell rather than snap, and leave real gaps between phrases — the bass should have its own breathing room, not fill every beat.

Step 5: Layer horns and vocal harmonies

Add a short horn line using a SoundFont brass patch, or record a vocal harmony part through your mic and comp the best take — see How to comp the best take from multiple recordings. Keep the horn or harmony part sparse: a two- or three-note stab at the top of the chorus, or a harmony line doubling the lead vocal on the hook, is usually enough. If you're recording a vocal, Tracks' built-in autotune can gently tighten pitch on a harmony part without making it sound processed — see How to use autotune in your browser.

Step 6: Arrange and export

Reggae usually follows a straightforward verse-chorus form built on repetition — the groove itself is the hook as much as any melody. A workable shape looks like this:

  1. Intro (4–8 bars): drums and skank only, establishing the one-drop before the bass enters.
  2. Verse (8–16 bars): full band groove under a vocal or lead melody, bass playing its full melodic part.
  3. Chorus (8 bars): horns or harmonies layer in on top of the full groove for lift.
  4. Bridge or instrumental break (8 bars): strip back to drums, skank, and bass, or feature the horn line alone.
  5. Final chorus and outro (8–16 bars): full arrangement, fading out on the groove rather than a hard stop.

Block these sections out on the Tracks timeline before polishing any individual part — see How to arrange a song for more on building a structure from loops, and Sidechain compression explained if you want the skank or horns to duck gently under the vocal.

When the balance feels right, export to WAV for a clean master file or MP3 to share online — see How to export your song to MP3 or WAV for the full walkthrough. Your project stays in your browser the entire time; Tracks never uploads it anywhere.

Frequently asked questions

What BPM is reggae?

Most reggae sits between 60 and 90 BPM, with classic roots reggae leaning slower and modern, dancehall-influenced reggae pushing closer to 90. 70 BPM is a reliable starting point.

What makes the one-drop different from other drum patterns?

Almost every other genre puts a strong hit on beat one. The one-drop deliberately leaves beat one empty and lands the kick and snare together on beat three instead, which is what gives reggae its distinctive lurch and lift.

Do I need a real guitar to play the skank?

No — you can program the skank in the Tracks piano roll using a guitar or organ SoundFont and get the same choppy, off-beat feel. What matters is short, staccato notes landing precisely on the off-beats, not the source instrument.

Why does my reggae track feel flat?

The most common cause is a bassline that just holds the root note instead of moving melodically. Reggae bass carries real melodic weight — try walking it between chord tones and giving it its own phrasing instead of treating it as a low-end anchor.
