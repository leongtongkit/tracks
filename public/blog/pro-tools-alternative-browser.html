A free Pro Tools alternative that runs in any browser

By Tongkit Leong · August 12, 2026 · 7 min read

Pro Tools is Avid's professional recording DAW, sold on a subscription (with a perpetual license on some tiers), and it's the standard in commercial studios and post-production houses. Tracks is a free browser DAW with real multitrack recording, take comping, and a full mixer, and it opens in a tab with no subscription, no install, and no account.

The core difference: subscription vs. tab

Pro Tools is desktop software for Windows and Mac, and getting the full feature set means paying for it on an ongoing basis. Avid sells it in tiers — Pro Tools Intro is a free, heavily limited entry point, Artist and Studio sit in the middle, and Ultimate adds surround mixing, video engine features, and support for Avid's own audio hardware at the top of the range. Most tiers renew as a subscription; stepping up a tier later usually means paying more per year, not a one-time top-up.

Tracks skips that structure entirely. There's no tier to choose and no renewal to track. You open tracks.jfound.net in Chrome, Firefox, Edge, or Safari — on Windows, Mac, Linux, or a Chromebook — and every feature is available immediately, for free, for as long as you want to keep using it.

Where Pro Tools is still the stronger tool

Pro Tools' reputation is built on recording, not beat-making, and that's where it still leads. It supports AAX plugin hosting, so a studio can load third-party compressors, EQs, and restoration tools from decades of professional plugin development, plus Avid's own HDX hardware for large sessions with dozens of simultaneous inputs and near-zero latency. Its editing tools — elastic audio, clip gain automation, and a session format that's the de facto exchange standard between studios — exist because Pro Tools was built around tracking bands and dialogue, at a scale Tracks isn't designed for.

If you're working in a commercial studio, scoring for picture, or need to hand a session off to an engineer at another facility, Pro Tools is still the tool everyone else expects to receive. That interoperability, plus its plugin ecosystem, is the real reason professionals keep paying for it.

Where Tracks is the better starting point

Cost and access

Tracks costs nothing to try or to keep using, with no subscription to start or cancel. If you just want to record a vocal, program a beat, and mix a track today without weighing Pro Tools Intro's track-count limits against a paid tier, that decision disappears.

Recording, without the interface requirement

Pro Tools historically works best with an Avid or third-party audio interface for reliable low-latency monitoring. Tracks records straight from whatever input the browser sees — a laptop mic, a USB mic, or an audio interface if you have one — with a count-in and take comping across multiple passes, the same core workflow Pro Tools built its name on, just without the hardware requirement. See recording guitar or any instrument in your browser for how that works with real instruments.

Instruments built in, no plugin shopping

Pro Tools ships with a modest stock instrument set and generally expects you to add third-party virtual instruments for serious sound design. Tracks includes a subtractive synth and an FM synth, 808/909-style drum machines, a 16-pad sampler, and SoundFont (.sf2) playback, all built in — see the best free VST alternative instruments in the browser. None of it needs a separate purchase or install.

Mixing depth without a plugin cart

Every track in Tracks has a parametric EQ, compressor, gate, and de-esser built in, plus reverb and delay sends, group buses, sidechain ducking, and automation on any parameter — see how to mix a track: EQ, compression, and reverb basics. Pro Tools ships stock dynamics and EQ plugins too, but a studio-grade chain there usually means adding AAX plugins per channel. In Tracks, the chain is already on every track.

Vocals and stems

Tracks includes built-in autotune, time-stretch/repitch, and stem extraction that splits an imported audio file into vocals, drums, bass, and other. Pro Tools can reach similar results through Melodyne integration or third-party plugins, but those are separate purchases layered on top of the DAW itself rather than something switched on by default.

Privacy and local storage

Tracks never uploads a project anywhere — everything, including recorded audio, stays on your device in local browser storage. Pro Tools is a local desktop application too, so on storage they're philosophically similar: neither requires cloud storage of your work by default, though Avid's Cloud Collaboration features are opt-in on top of the desktop app. See where your project actually lives for how Tracks' local-only storage works.

Which one should you use?

Choose Pro Tools if

You work in or with a commercial studio, need to exchange sessions with other engineers who standardize on Pro Tools, or rely on AAX plugins or Avid hardware for large-scale tracking. It's also the stronger fit for post-production and dialogue work where its editing tools were purpose-built for that job.

Choose Tracks if

You want to record, comp, and mix a track today without a subscription, an install, or an account, and you don't need third-party plugin hosting or studio-to-studio session interchange. Tracks covers the recording, instruments, mixing, and export workflow most independent musicians and producers actually use, and because it's local-only, your audio never leaves your device either way.

Frequently asked questions

Is Pro Tools free to use?

Avid offers Pro Tools Intro as a free, track-limited entry tier; the fuller Artist, Studio, and Ultimate tiers are paid subscriptions (with a perpetual option on some). Tracks has no tier or expiration — every feature is free every time you open the tab.

Can Tracks load AAX or VST plugins like Pro Tools can?

No. Browsers don't support hosting native AAX or VST/AU plugins, so Tracks ships its instruments and effects built into the engine instead. If a specific third-party plugin is central to your workflow, Pro Tools is the path to using it.

Does Tracks support multitrack recording like Pro Tools?

Yes. Add as many audio tracks as you need, arm them, and record with a count-in. Tracks also supports take comping — recording several passes of the same part and combining the best moments into one performance, the same idea Pro Tools built its editing reputation on.

Can I move a project between Pro Tools and Tracks?

Not directly — Pro Tools sessions and Tracks project files (.tracks.json) are different formats, and neither reads the other's native file. You can bridge between them with standard interchange formats: export a standard MIDI file from Tracks (see how to export a MIDI file) to bring notes into Pro Tools, or export stems or a WAV/MP3 mixdown to move finished audio the other way.

Is Tracks good enough to finish a full track without Pro Tools?

Yes. Tracks covers recording, instruments, mixing, mastering-level loudness tools, and WAV/MP3/stem/MIDI export — the full path from a blank project to a finished, exported song, as covered in how to make a song in your browser for beginners. Whether it's "enough" depends only on whether you need the AAX plugins, Avid hardware, or studio session interchange that Pro Tools offers and Tracks doesn't.
