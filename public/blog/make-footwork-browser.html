How to make footwork in your browser

By Tongkit Leong · August 27, 2026 · 8 min read

Footwork is built by chopping a vocal into a short, stuttering loop, locking a sparse kick pattern around it, and layering rapid triplet hi-hats on top at around 160 BPM — and you can put the whole thing together from scratch in the Tracks browser studio in an afternoon, with no software to install and no account to create.

The anatomy of a footwork track

Footwork grew out of Chicago's juke and ghetto house scenes as music for competitive dance battles, and its defining trait is speed paired with restraint: a fast tempo built from small, repeating cells rather than a wall of sound. A handful of elements show up in nearly every footwork track:

  • A chopped, stuttering vocal loop — a short vocal phrase cut into fragments and reordered into a repeating, glitchy hook.
  • A sparse, syncopated kick — a minimal 808-style kick pattern that leaves gaps rather than filling every beat.
  • Rapid triplet hi-hats — fast, off-kilter hi-hat patterns that push against the kick and give the track its restless feel.
  • A deep, simple sub bass — a low, mostly static bassline that anchors the track without adding melodic clutter.
  • Short repeating cells — four- or eight-bar loops that mutate gradually instead of building toward a single drop.

Tracks covers this end to end — the sampler for the vocal chop, the drum machine for the kick and hi-hat pattern, and the subtractive synth for the sub bass.

Step 1: Set your tempo

Open the Tracks studio and set the BPM in the transport bar. Footwork sits well above most club tempos:

  • Most footwork: 155–160 BPM. The pocket where the triplet hi-hats and chopped vocal feel frantic but still danceable.
  • Some tracks: as low as 150 or as high as 165 BPM.

160 BPM is a safe default and the tempo most associated with the genre.

Step 2: Chop a vocal into a stuttering loop

Load a vocal sample — a spoken phrase, a sung line, an acapella — into the Tracks 16-pad sampler and chop it into short fragments, ideally single words or even syllables. See How to chop and flip a sample for the sampler workflow.

The footwork move is repetition with small edits: take a fragment and repeat it two, three, or four times before moving to the next one, cutting some repeats short for a stutter. Reorder fragments across the pads until the loop feels like a rhythmic hook rather than the original sentence.

Keep the loop short

One or two bars is usually enough. Footwork vocal hooks work because they repeat obsessively within a tight window, not because they carry a long phrase — the stutter itself is the melody.

Step 3: Program a sparse, syncopated kick pattern

Add a track and open the Tracks drum machine. Program a minimal 808-style kick — a handful of hits per bar placed off the obvious downbeats, leaving real silence between them. See How to lock a bassline to the kick for keeping the low end tight once the bass comes in.

Resist the urge to fill every gap. The space between kicks is what lets the chopped vocal read clearly, and it's what separates footwork's sparse pocket from denser, four-on-the-floor styles.

Step 4: Layer rapid triplet hi-hats

Program a fast hi-hat pattern in triplet groupings across the bar, mixing single hits with quick rolls. See Quantize and swing explained for working with non-straight subdivisions in the piano roll and drum grid.

The hi-hats should feel like they're fighting the kick pattern rather than reinforcing it — that tension between the sparse low end and the busy top end is a large part of what makes footwork feel restless and fast even at a tempo that isn't extreme.

Step 5: Add a deep, minimal sub bass

Open the subtractive synth and write a simple bassline that mostly holds a single root note, moving only to mark section changes. See Subtractive and FM sound design explained for shaping a clean, deep sub tone with the filter closed down and minimal harmonic content.

Keep it steady underneath the chopped vocal and hi-hats. The bass in footwork is a floor to stand on, not another rhythmic element competing for attention.

Step 6: Arrange and export

Footwork arrangements are built from short cells that repeat and mutate rather than a traditional build-and-drop structure. A workable shape looks like this:

  1. Intro (4–8 bars): kick and hi-hats alone, establishing the groove before the vocal enters.
  2. Cell A (8–16 bars): the full pattern — kick, hi-hats, vocal chop, and bass — locked in together.
  3. Cell B (8–16 bars): a variation on the vocal chop or a new fragment, keeping the drums mostly the same.
  4. Break (4–8 bars): strip back to just the vocal chop or just the drums for contrast.
  5. Return and outro (8–16 bars): bring the full pattern back, then thin it out again to close.

Block these sections out on the Tracks timeline before polishing any individual part — see How to arrange a song for more on building a structure from loops, and How to make Jersey club in your browser for another vocal-chop-driven style built at a similarly frantic tempo.

When the balance feels right, export to WAV for a clean master file or MP3 to share online — see How to export your song to MP3 or WAV for the full walkthrough. Your project stays in your browser the entire time; Tracks never uploads it anywhere.

Frequently asked questions

What BPM is footwork?

Most footwork sits between 155 and 160 BPM, with some tracks ranging from 150 up to 165. 160 BPM is the most common starting point.

What's the difference between footwork and juke?

Juke came first as a faster, more dancefloor-oriented offshoot of Chicago ghetto house, and footwork grew out of juke specifically to soundtrack competitive dance battles, with sparser drums and more aggressive, chopped vocal sampling. The production techniques overlap heavily, and this guide's approach works for either.

Why does my footwork track feel too busy?

If the pattern feels cluttered, check the kick first — footwork relies on real gaps in the low end so the vocal chop and hi-hats have room to read. Adding space to the kick usually fixes a busy-feeling track faster than removing other layers.

Do I need vocal samples to make footwork?

No — you can chop any short audio source the same way, including your own recordings or synthesized phrases. What defines the sound is the stutter-and-repeat chopping technique, not the source material itself.
