How to finish a song: stop looping and get to a done track

By Tongkit Leong · October 15, 2026 · 7 min read

To finish a song, stop polishing the loop and move through fixed stages: build the idea, arrange it into a full structure, mix it, export it, and call it done. Setting a time limit for each stage in the free Tracks browser studio is what turns an eight-bar loop into a finished track.

Why songs stall at the loop

Almost every producer has a folder of great eight-bar loops and very few finished songs. The loop is the fun part: you hear it back immediately and it sounds good on repeat. The harder work comes after, when you have to decide what happens next, and that is where momentum dies.

Finishing is a separate skill from starting. It mostly comes down to making decisions earlier and accepting "good enough" at each stage.

Step 1: Make the idea, then stop (30 to 60 minutes)

Open the studio, pick a tempo, and build one section: a drum pattern, a bass part, and a melody or chord loop. If you need a starting point, pick a tempo by genre. Do not mix yet. When the loop makes you nod your head, move on, even if you think it could be better.

Step 2: Arrange it into a full song (1 to 2 hours)

This is the stage that decides whether you finish. Copy your loop along the timeline and create a simple structure before you tweak any sounds. A basic layout is intro, verse, chorus, verse, chorus, outro. Use song structure and arrangement as your map.

Then make the sections different from each other:

  • Mute the drums or bass in the verses so the chorus feels bigger.
  • Add a riser or a short break before the chorus; the buildup and drop guide shows how.
  • Drop a part for a bar to create contrast, then bring it back.
  • Use the markers in the arrange view to label each section so you can see the whole song at a glance.

Aim for a rough but complete song from start to end. A complete rough song beats a perfect half song every time.

Step 3: Mix in short passes (1 hour)

Now you can mix, because you finally know what the whole song contains. Work in passes instead of fiddling endlessly: one pass for levels, one for EQ, one for effects. The mixing basics guide covers EQ, compression, and reverb, and gain staging keeps you from clipping the master.

Compare against a commercial song you like using the reference track method. It gives you a target, which stops you from tweaking without an end point.

Step 4: Export and listen away from the studio (30 minutes)

Export a WAV or MP3 and listen to it on your phone, in earbuds, in the car, anywhere but your editing screen. Take notes on the three biggest problems and ignore the rest. Details of the formats are in how to export your song to MP3 or WAV.

Step 5: Fix three things, then release

Go back, fix only the three problems from your notes, and export again. Then stop. A song does not have to be perfect to be finished, and the next one will be better because you completed this one.

Habits that keep you finishing

  • Set a deadline. A two-day limit forces decisions. Without one, you will keep adjusting forever.
  • Limit your sounds. Choose a handful of instruments up front. Endless browsing is a way of avoiding the arrangement.
  • Save checkpoints. Your project stays on your device, so download a .tracks.json copy at the end of each session. It is a self-contained file you can reopen later.
  • Arrange before you polish. Sound design on a loop you never finish is wasted effort.
  • Finish small first. A short two-minute track that is complete teaches more than a long one that is not.

Frequently asked questions

How long should it take to finish a song?

For a beginner, a few focused hours spread over one or two days is realistic. Fast tracks get done because the maker kept moving instead of perfecting each part.

How do I know when a song is done?

When it has a beginning, middle, and end, the levels are balanced on a few different speakers, and your remaining notes are small preferences rather than real problems.

What if I run out of ideas for the next section?

Reuse what you have with variation: change the drum pattern, remove an instrument, transpose the chords, or change the tempo section. Contrast does more than brand-new material.

Should I mix as I go or at the end?

Rough levels while you build are fine. Do the serious mixing after the arrangement is complete, because changes to the structure change the mix.

Can I do all of this in the browser?

Yes. The Tracks studio has a multi-track timeline, a piano roll, built-in instruments and drums, mixing tools, and WAV or MP3 export, and nothing leaves your device. Start with how to make a song in your browser if you are new.
