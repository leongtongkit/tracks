Panning and stereo width explained: how to make a mix feel wide

By Tongkit Leong · September 11, 2026 · 7 min read

Panning moves a sound left or right between the two speakers, and stereo width is the result of panning several sounds to different positions so the whole mix feels spread out instead of piled up in the middle. Here is how to use both in Tracks to make a mix feel wide without losing anything when it plays back in mono.

What panning actually does

Every track in Tracks has a pan control that sits between its volume fader and the master bus. At center, a track plays equally out of the left and right speaker. Turn it left or right and the balance between the two speakers shifts, so the sound seems to come from a position somewhere between them instead of dead center. Nothing about the sound itself changes — no EQ, no volume — only where it appears to sit in the stereo image.

That's a different tool from level or frequency. Two sounds fighting for the same space in a mix can be separated three ways: pull one down in volume, carve out room with EQ, or move one of them to a different spot in the stereo field. Panning is often the cheapest fix, because it doesn't cost you any level or tone — it just gives two competing sounds their own lane.

Why stereo width matters

A mix with every track panned to center sounds narrow and congested, because every instrument is stacked on top of every other instrument in the exact same spot. Spread supporting elements across the stereo field and the same instruments suddenly have room to breathe — a doubled rhythm guitar sitting hard left doesn't need to duck out of the way of a synth pad sitting hard right, because they were never in the same place to begin with.

Width also gives a mix a sense of scale. A chorus that pushes doubled vocals, extra guitar layers, or wide pads out to the edges reads as bigger than a verse that keeps everything closer to center — the stereo field itself becomes part of the arrangement, the same way automation makes a mix move over time.

A practical panning method

Rather than panning by feel and hoping it holds together, work from the center out:

  • Keep the low end centered. Kick, bass, and any sub-heavy 808 stay at center. Bass frequencies carry most of a song's energy, and panning them off to one side makes that speaker do all the work while the other sits comparatively empty — it also causes real problems the moment the mix gets folded to mono.
  • Keep the lead centered too. The lead vocal or main melody is the thing a listener locks onto, and it should stay strong and stable no matter which speaker they're standing closer to. Snare usually stays centered alongside it for the same reason.
  • Pan supporting layers apart. Doubled rhythm guitars, background vocal stacks, layered synth pads, and extra hi-hat or percussion layers are exactly the parts that build width. Send one doubled take left and its pair right, or spread three synth layers across left, center, and right, and the arrangement stops sounding like a single wall of sound.
  • Use small moves before big ones. A pair of background vocals at 30% left and right usually reads as wider and more natural than the same pair hard-panned to the extremes. Save full hard-left/hard-right panning for parts that are meant to be a clearly separate texture, not the main body of the arrangement.

In the Tracks mixer, each channel strip's pan dial shows the position as C for center or L/R with a percentage, so you can see at a glance how a track sits relative to everything around it — useful once a project has a dozen tracks and it's easy to lose track of who's panned where.

Panning to fix a crowded mix

When two instruments are stepping on each other, it's tempting to reach straight for EQ. But if they occupy similar frequency ranges and similar stereo positions, moving one of them in the stereo field can solve the collision with less surgery than carving frequencies out of both. A rhythm guitar and a synth pad sitting in the same midrange, both centered, will mask each other no matter how much you EQ them apart — pan one left and one right and they suddenly have their own space, on top of whatever EQ separation you still want. See the EQ cheat sheet by instrument for the frequency side of that same problem, and use panning as the other half of the toolkit.

Automating pan for movement

Because pan is one of the parameters you can automate on any track, it doesn't have to be a single fixed position for the whole song. A vocal ad-lib that sweeps from left to right on its way out, a riser that pans wider as it builds into a drop, or a fill that ping-pongs between speakers on the way into a chorus are all just pan automation drawn as a curve instead of set as a static dial position. Use it sparingly — constant movement on every track reads as gimmicky fast, while one well-placed sweep on a transition or ad-lib draws the ear exactly where you want it. See how to use automation to make your mix move for how to draw and edit automation curves in Tracks.

Checking your mix in mono

Stereo width only exists on a system with two separated speakers. A phone speaker, a single Bluetooth speaker, and a lot of PA systems sum the left and right channels into one — and anything panned in a way that relies on separation can behave differently once that happens. Two identical sounds panned hard left and hard right will sum fine, but a sound that was deliberately made wider with a stereo effect can lose level or start to sound thin once it's folded down. Get in the habit of soloing a section and comparing it against a mono reference before you consider a mix finished, the same way you'd check gain staging headroom before mastering — see gain staging: how to avoid clipping and keep a healthy mix for the level side of that same final pass.

Common mistakes

  • Panning the kick or bass off center. It throws off the balance between speakers and causes problems the moment the mix sums to mono. Keep low end centered.
  • Hard-panning everything to the extremes. A mix where every track sits at full left or full right loses the sense of a coherent stereo image — it reads as scattered rather than wide. Reserve the extremes for parts meant to stand apart.
  • Never checking mono. A mix that only gets auditioned in stereo headphones can hide problems that show up the moment it plays through a laptop speaker or a phone.
  • Using panning to fix a level problem. If two sounds are clashing because one is simply too loud, panning them apart just hides the symptom. Fix the balance first, then use panning to build width on top of a mix that already sits right.

Frequently asked questions

Should drums be panned at all, or kept centered?

Kick and snare usually stay centered since they anchor the beat and the low end. Overhead cymbals, toms, and hi-hats are commonly panned to roughly match where they'd sit around a real drum kit, which adds a natural sense of width to a drum bus without touching the core of the groove.

What's the difference between panning and a stereo widener effect?

Panning moves a single mono source to a position in the stereo field — it's still one sound with one location. A stereo widener processes a signal to make it occupy more of the stereo field than a simple pan position can, often at the cost of mono compatibility if pushed too far. Panning individual tracks apart is usually the safer and more predictable way to build width across a full mix.

Why does my mix sound different on my phone than in Tracks?

Phone speakers typically play back close to mono, and any stereo effect built purely through panning depends on two separated speakers to be heard the way it was intended. That's exactly why checking a mono fold-down matters — see the mono section above.

Can I do this in the Tracks browser studio?

Yes. Every track and bus in the Tracks mixer has a pan dial, pan is one of the parameters you can automate over time, and you can solo any section to check how it sits before moving on. Open the studio and start spreading a mix out — nothing is uploaded, your project stays on your device.
