How to mix on headphones: get a balanced mix without studio monitors

By Tongkit Leong · October 14, 2026 · 7 min read

Yes, you can mix on headphones: keep the volume moderate, check your low end against a commercial reference track, listen in mono, and test the result on at least one other speaker before you call it done. The free Tracks browser studio has everything you need for that routine, with nothing to install.

Why headphones mislead you

Headphones put each channel straight into one ear. That has three effects you need to know about before you trust what you hear.

  • Stereo feels wider than it is. Nothing from the left reaches your right ear, so panned parts sound more separated than they will on speakers.
  • Bass is easy to misjudge. Many headphones boost the low end, and you do not feel bass in your chest the way you do in a room. Mixes made on bass-heavy headphones often come out thin elsewhere.
  • Ear fatigue arrives fast. The drivers sit centimeters from your ears, so loud sessions tire you out and your judgement drifts.

None of this is a dealbreaker. It just means you replace the room with a few habits.

Step 1: Set a safe, steady level

Pick one comfortable listening level and leave your headphone volume alone for the whole session. Louder always sounds better for the first few seconds, which is why changing volume mid-mix leads to bad decisions. If you want to judge how loud a part really is, turn it down and check that it still reads. Quiet playback also shows you balance problems that loud playback hides.

Step 2: Get your levels right first

Before reaching for EQ, set the faders so the drums, bass, and lead sit where you want them. Level balance fixes more problems than any effect. Leave headroom on the master so nothing clips; the gain staging guide shows how.

Step 3: Use a reference track

A reference track is the best replacement for a calibrated room. Import a finished song you love in a similar style, play it through the same headphones, and match how your mix compares: how much bass there is, how bright the hi-hats are, how loud the vocal sits. Because both tracks go through the same headphones, their coloration cancels out. See how to mix with a reference track for the full method.

Step 4: Check the low end carefully

Low frequencies are where headphones deceive you most. Compare your kick and bass to the reference track, not to what feels right. If your bass sounds huge next to the reference, pull it back. If it sounds weak, resist the urge to boost it by ear alone. Use the per-track EQ to remove rumble with a high-pass filter on parts that do not need deep bass, which tightens the whole mix. If the result sounds cloudy, fixing a muddy mix covers the 200 to 400 Hz range.

Step 5: Listen in mono

Headphones flatter wide stereo, so check that your mix survives being folded to mono. Most phones and computers have a mono audio option in their accessibility settings. Switch it on and play your bounce. If the lead vocal, snare, or bass drops in volume or sounds hollow, something is fighting itself across the left and right channels. Pan less extreme, or reconsider stereo widening effects. The panning and stereo width guide explains why.

Step 6: Test on other speakers

Export a reference bounce and play it on everything you have: laptop speakers, a phone, earbuds, a car, a Bluetooth speaker. Each one shows a different weakness. Write down what you notice, go back to the project, and adjust. You are looking for a mix that is acceptable everywhere rather than perfect on one device. Export a WAV for testing; how to export your song to MP3 or WAV covers the options.

Habits that help

  • Take breaks. Rest your ears for five to ten minutes every hour. Fresh ears hear problems immediately.
  • Mix in short passes. Do one pass for levels, one for EQ, one for effects, then stop and come back the next day.
  • Prefer open-back or neutral headphones if you can, but any pair works if you use a reference track.
  • Do not chase small differences. If a change is hard to hear, it probably does not matter.
  • Save a project file before big changes. Your project stays on your device, so download a .tracks.json copy as a checkpoint.

Frequently asked questions

Can you really mix a song on headphones?

Yes. Many released tracks were mixed or at least finished on headphones. The key is to compare against reference tracks and test on several playback systems.

Are earbuds okay for mixing?

They work in a pinch, but they usually lack accurate bass and can hide problems. Use them as one of your test systems rather than your main mixing tool.

Why does my mix sound different on my phone?

Phone speakers cannot reproduce deep bass, so anything below roughly 150 Hz disappears and quiet parts can vanish. If the song still works there, the midrange balance is probably sound.

How loud should I mix at?

Moderate. If you can comfortably talk over the music, you are in a good range for long sessions and for judging balance.
