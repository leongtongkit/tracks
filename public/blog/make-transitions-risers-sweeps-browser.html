How to make risers, sweeps, and impacts for song transitions in your browser

By Tongkit Leong · November 21, 2026 · 6 min read

A transition is a short passage, usually one to four bars, that tells the listener a new section is coming. You can build one for free in the Tracks browser studio with an automated filter sweep, a drum fill, a moment of silence and a hit on the first beat of the next section.

What risers, sweeps and impacts do

Songs are built from sections, and the join between two sections is where listeners notice a change. A transition makes that join feel planned instead of abrupt.

  • Riser: a sound that climbs in pitch, brightness or volume to build tension.
  • Sweep: a filter or pitch movement across a few bars. A riser is often just a sweep.
  • Impact: a single heavy hit, such as a crash, a low drum or a bass note, that marks the start of the new section.
  • Gap: a brief silence or reverb tail that makes the next hit feel bigger.

You do not need all four. One well-placed move is better than a pile of effects.

How to build a transition in Tracks

Step 1: Pick the join

Open the studio and find the point where one section ends and another begins, for example the last bars of a verse before the chorus. Add a timeline marker at the start of the new section so you can see exactly where the impact belongs. Aim for one or two bars of transition. See song structure and arrangement if you are still deciding where sections go.

Step 2: Sweep a filter with automation

The simplest riser is an existing sound that slowly opens up. Tracks lets you automate any parameter, so draw a line that raises a filter cutoff over the last two to four bars of the section. The part gets brighter and louder to the ear without you adding anything new. You can automate the same way on a synth pad, a chord stab or a whole drum bus. The basics are in automation in your mix.

Step 3: Write a riser on the synth

For a dedicated riser, use the built-in synth with a long held note and automate its filter or pitch upward across the transition. Use a bright, sustained patch, let it climb, and cut it off right where the new section starts. If you have a riser or noise recording of your own, load it into the sampler and place it on the timeline. Synth sound design covers the filter and envelope controls.

Step 4: Tighten the drums

A drum fill is the most natural transition there is. In the last bar, program a snare roll that gets denser, for instance quarter notes, then eighths, then sixteenths, and raise the velocity as it goes. How to program drum fills walks through patterns. Taking the hi-hats out for the final bar also tells the ear that something is about to change.

Step 5: Leave a gap

Silence is a strong tool. Mute everything for the last beat, or let a reverb tail from the final note ring into the new section. Send the last hit to a reverb send and automate it so the tail is long on that one note only. More on that in reverb and delay explained.

Step 6: Land the impact

On the first beat of the new section, place a crash, a low drum hit or a bass note. The 808 and 909 drum machines have the usual crash and low-end sounds, and the sampler can hold your own. Layer it with the kick and check that the two do not fight; a bassline that locks with the kick helps here.

Common transition recipes

  • Verse to chorus: snare roll, hi-hats out, one beat of silence, crash and full band on the downbeat.
  • Breakdown to drop: filter sweep on the pad, rising noise or synth, a bar of near silence, then the bass and kick together. See buildup and drop explained.
  • Chorus to verse: the opposite move. Let the reverb tail ring, then start the verse with fewer parts.
  • Intro to first verse: a short fill and one part dropping out, as in writing a song intro.

Common mistakes

  • Making the riser too loud. It should build the section, not cover it. Check it against the rest of the mix.
  • Missing the bar line. A riser that ends a little late sounds like a mistake. Zoom in and line the end up with the downbeat.
  • Using the same transition every time. Vary the length and the move so the song stays interesting.
  • Letting the low end clash. A riser full of bass can fight the kick. Cut the lows with a high-pass filter.

Frequently asked questions

What is a riser in music production?

A riser is a sound that rises in pitch, brightness or volume to build tension before a new section, such as a chorus or a drop.

How long should a transition be?

One to two bars suits most songs. Dance music often uses four to eight bars for a longer build.

Can I make a riser without a sample pack?

Yes. Automate a filter or pitch on the built-in synth, or sweep a filter on a sound you already have.

Is Tracks free?

Yes. Tracks is free and runs in your browser.

Does my music get uploaded?

No. Your projects stay on your device.
