How to write a song intro that hooks listeners in 15 seconds

By Tongkit Leong · November 20, 2026 · 6 min read

A good song intro states the hook or sets the mood in the first 8 to 16 bars, then gets out of the way so the verse can start. You can build one in the Tracks browser studio for free, with nothing to install, using the timeline, the piano roll and the built-in drums and synths.

What an intro is for

An intro does two jobs. It tells the listener what kind of song this is, and it creates a little tension that the first verse or chorus can release. Everything else is optional.

Most listeners decide quickly whether to stay. A long, slow wind-up that never says anything about the song gives them time to leave. A short intro that plays the strongest idea straight away gives them a reason to stay.

Three intro styles that work

Hook first

Play the main melody or chord loop alone, then start the verse over the same material. This is the safest choice because the listener hears the best idea before anything else. It suits pop, lo-fi and most beat-driven genres.

Build from nothing

Start with one quiet part and add a new one every two or four bars until the full band arrives. This works for dance music, ambient and cinematic pieces. See buildup and drop explained for the tension side of it.

Drums or bass only

Open with just the groove. It is easy to write, easy to loop and gives a producer or DJ something to mix into. It pairs well with a bassline that locks with the kick.

How to write an intro in Tracks

Step 1: Pick the one idea

Open the studio and decide what the intro will announce: a chord loop, a melody or a drum groove. If you have none yet, write one in the piano roll first. Chord progressions in the piano roll is a quick start.

Step 2: Start with one or two parts

Put that idea on its own track, perhaps with a soft pad or a simple beat underneath. Resist adding more yet. The point is to leave room to build.

Step 3: Add a part every two or four bars

Place new clips on the timeline so each one enters on a bar line. A common order is drums, then bass, then a lead or vocal chop. Every entrance gives the ear something new without any change in volume.

Step 4: Signal the verse

In the last bar of the intro, drop a part out or add a short drum fill. That small gap makes the verse feel like an arrival. How to program drum fills shows how to build one. Add a timeline marker at the start of the verse so you can jump to it.

Step 5: Trim it

Play the song from the start and ask whether each bar earns its place. For most songs 8 to 16 bars is enough. If you are unsure, cut it shorter and see whether the song feels better.

How long should an intro be?

  • Pop and rap: a few seconds to about 15 seconds, often just the hook or a short tag.
  • Lo-fi and chill beats: 4 to 8 bars is usual.
  • Dance and electronic: 16 to 32 bars is normal because DJs need room to mix.
  • Cinematic and ambient: as long as the mood needs, since the listener expects a slow start.

These are habits, not rules. The test is whether the listener is still interested when the verse arrives.

Common mistakes

  • Saving the best idea for the chorus only. Hint at it in the intro.
  • Adding everything at once. A full-band start leaves nowhere to build.
  • An intro that has no link to the song. Reuse the chords, melody or sound from later sections.
  • Never finishing. Set a bar count and stick to it. More on this in how to finish a song.

Frequently asked questions

What makes a good song intro?

It is short, it uses an idea from the song itself, and it builds toward the first verse or chorus instead of delaying it.

Should an intro have vocals?

It can. A short vocal tag or chopped phrase makes a strong opener, but plenty of songs use instruments only.

How many bars should an intro be?

Eight bars is a good default. Go shorter for pop, longer for dance music.
