A free Reaper alternative that runs in any browser

By Tongkit Leong · September 27, 2026 · 7 min read

Reaper is a paid, install-only DAW you download and license once, prized for being cheap, lightweight, and endlessly customizable. Tracks is a free browser DAW with a piano roll, step sequencer, built-in synths, and a full mixer that opens in a tab with no purchase, no install, and no account.

The core difference: install vs. tab

Reaper is desktop software for Windows, Mac, and Linux. You download an installer — a famously small one, often just a few dozen megabytes — and run a 60-day evaluation that unlocks every feature with no functional limits. After the trial period, Reaper doesn't lock you out or disable saving; it shows a nag screen reminding you to buy a license, which costs $60 for personal use under $20,000 in annual revenue, or $225 for a full commercial license. Nothing stops you from continuing to use the evaluation copy indefinitely, but the license is the honest, intended path.

Tracks skips the download and the license question entirely. There is no installer, no evaluation period, and no nag screen. You open tracks.jfound.net in Chrome, Firefox, Edge, or Safari, and every feature — synths, drum machines, mixer, automation, export — is available immediately, for free, on any device that runs a modern browser.

Where Reaper is still the stronger tool

Reaper's biggest advantage over any browser DAW, including Tracks, is full VST/VST3/AU/CLAP plugin hosting. You can load third-party synths, effects, and mastering plugins from the entire commercial and free plugin market and route them through Reaper's flexible mixer and unlimited buses. Tracks does not support external plugins; every instrument and effect is built into the engine itself. If your workflow depends on a specific third-party plugin, Reaper is the far more capable option.

Reaper is also known for how deeply customizable it is. Its interface, toolbars, and color themes can be rebuilt from scratch, and its scripting language, ReaScript (Lua, EEL2, or Python), lets producers write their own actions, automate repetitive editing tasks, or build entire custom tools inside the DAW. Its routing matrix is nearly unlimited — you can send audio anywhere, in any combination, which power users rely on for complex parallel processing chains. None of that customization exists in Tracks, which trades it for a simpler, fixed workflow that works the same for everyone out of the box.

One area where Reaper is actually thinner than you might expect: it ships with very few built-in instruments — a basic synth (ReaSynth) and a simple sample player (ReaSamplomatic5000). Most Reaper users add third-party instrument plugins to get real sound design or sampling depth, which brings its own cost and setup time.

Where Tracks is the better starting point

Cost and access

Tracks costs nothing to try or to keep using, with no evaluation period and no license to buy later. If you're deciding whether music production is worth investing money in at all, or you just need to finish one track without buying software, that removes the decision entirely.

Instruments built in, no plugin shopping required

Tracks ships a subtractive synth and an FM synth for building your own patches from oscillators and filters, 808/909-style drum machines, a 16-pad sampler, and SoundFont (.sf2) playback — see the best free VST alternative instruments in the browser for a full rundown. Where Reaper hands you a blank mixer and expects you to bring your own instrument plugins, Tracks gives you a usable instrument set the moment the tab loads.

A piano roll and step sequencer with no setup

Reaper's MIDI editor is powerful but general-purpose, since Reaper isn't built around any one workflow. Tracks' drum machine is a dedicated step sequencer — a grid you click to program a beat, with swing and velocity per step — and its piano roll supports velocity editing, quantize, swing, and a tempo map, covering the core of what most producers actually use a piano roll for day to day.

Mixing depth without a routing diagram

Every track in Tracks has a parametric EQ, compressor, gate, and de-esser built in, plus reverb and delay sends, group buses, sidechain ducking, and automation on any parameter — see how to mix a track: EQ, compression, and reverb basics. Reaper can match or exceed this with its bundled ReaPlugs and unlimited routing, but you have to build that chain yourself, track by track. In Tracks, it's already there on every channel.

Recording, vocals, and stems

Tracks records mic input with count-in and take comping, and a MIDI keyboard over Web MIDI with no drivers. It includes built-in autotune, time-stretch/repitch, and stem extraction that splits an imported audio file into vocals, drums, bass, and other. Reaper can record and edit audio at a professional level too, but pitch correction and stem separation usually mean adding a third-party plugin rather than a feature that's simply on by default.

Privacy and local storage

Tracks never uploads a project anywhere — everything, including recorded audio, stays on your device in local browser storage. Reaper is a local desktop application too, so on that point they're philosophically similar: neither one requires cloud storage of your work. See where your project actually lives for how Tracks' local-only storage works and how to back up a project file.

Which one should you use?

Choose Reaper if

You want to build a workflow around specific third-party plugins, you like customizing your DAW's interface and behavior down to the keystroke, or you're working on Linux and need a genuinely native option there. Its low price and unlimited routing also make it a favorite for producers who outgrow a simpler tool but don't want to pay for a more expensive flagship DAW.

Choose Tracks if

You want to start making music today without buying anything, installing anything, or creating an account, and you don't need third-party plugin hosting or custom scripting. Tracks covers the instruments, mixing, recording, and export workflow most producers actually use, and because it's local-only, your audio never leaves your device either way.

Frequently asked questions

Is Reaper actually free?

Reaper's evaluation period is fully functional for 60 days, and even after that it keeps working with an occasional reminder screen rather than a hard lockout. The license — $60 for personal use, $225 commercial — is how Reaper's developers ask you to pay for continued, licensed use. Tracks has no evaluation period or license question at all; it's free from the first tab you open.

Can Tracks load VST plugins like Reaper can?

No. Browsers don't support hosting native VST/AU/CLAP plugins, so Tracks — like any browser DAW — ships its instruments and effects built into the engine instead. If a specific third-party plugin is central to your sound, Reaper (or another desktop DAW with plugin hosting) is the only path to using it.

Does Tracks have anything like Reaper's ReaScript customization?

No. Reaper's ReaScript lets you write custom actions and tools in Lua, EEL2, or Python, which is a big part of why power users pick it. Tracks has a fixed interface and workflow with no scripting layer — it trades that flexibility for a studio that works the same, out of the box, for everyone.

Can I move a project between Reaper and Tracks?

Not directly — Reaper project files (.rpp) and Tracks project files (.tracks.json) are different formats, and neither reads the other's native file. You can bridge between them with standard interchange formats: export a standard MIDI file from Tracks (see how to export a MIDI file) to bring notes into Reaper, or export stems or a WAV/MP3 mixdown to move finished audio the other way.

Is Tracks good enough to finish a full track without Reaper?

Yes. Tracks covers arrangement, instruments, recording, mixing, mastering-level loudness tools, and WAV/MP3/stem/MIDI export — the full path from a blank timeline to a finished, exported song, as covered in how to make a song in your browser for beginners. Whether it's "enough" depends only on whether you need the third-party plugins or scripting Reaper supports and Tracks doesn't.
