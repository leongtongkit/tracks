How to make cinematic music in your browser

By Tongkit Leong · October 26, 2026 · 7 min read

Cinematic music is a low drone, slow string chords, a short repeating motif, and a long build that ends in one big hit — and you can write all of it in the Tracks browser studio for free, with no download and no account.

What makes music sound cinematic

Film-style cues rely on contrast and patience rather than a groove. Most of them share the same parts:

  • A drone — one low sustained note that gives the whole cue weight.
  • Slow chords — strings or pads playing whole notes, spread wide, changing every one or two bars.
  • A small motif — two or three notes that come back, so the listener has something to follow.
  • A build — layers, volume, and brightness all rising together.
  • A hit and a pause — a loud low impact, followed by silence or a long reverb tail.

In Tracks you get these from the subtractive and FM synths, SoundFont (.sf2) playback for strings and piano, the drum machines for impacts, reverb sends, and automation on any parameter.

Step 1: Set a slow tempo and a key

Set the BPM between 60 and 90. Pick a minor or modal key, such as D minor or A Dorian, and keep every part inside it. If you want the notes to sit in a particular key later, transposing in the browser is a quick fix.

Step 2: Lay a low drone

Add a synth track with a soft saw or triangle wave and close the low-pass filter until the tone is dark and round. Hold one low note, such as a low D, for the whole cue. Keep it quiet; it should be felt more than heard.

Step 3: Write slow string chords

Load a strings or pad SoundFont on a new track, or use a synth with a long attack and release. Write whole-note chords in the piano roll, one or two bars each. Spread the notes wide: a low root and fifth, then the third and an octave higher. For ideas on moving between chords, see chord progressions in the piano roll.

Slow, sparse progressions work best. Try i, VI, III, VII in a minor key and hold each chord for two bars.

Step 4: Add a simple motif

On a piano, celesta, or cello SoundFont, write a phrase of two or three notes and repeat it every four bars, changing one note each time. Keep it high or low, away from the string chords, with lots of space between repeats. Give it a reverb and delay send so it trails off.

Step 5: Build tension

Over the last 8 to 16 bars before the hit, stack changes that all point upward:

  1. Open the drone's filter cutoff with an automation lane.
  2. Raise the strings' volume gradually, also with automation.
  3. Add a snare or drum-machine roll that starts quiet and gets louder, and write its velocities rising in the piano roll.
  4. Add a riser from a synth: a noise or saw sweep whose pitch and filter climb.

The same idea drives build-ups and drops in dance music; in a cinematic cue you just do it slower and with acoustic-style sounds. Automation in your mix covers drawing the curves.

Step 6: Land one big hit

On the downbeat after the build, cut every layer for a moment. Then bring in a low drum, such as a layered 808 or 909 kick, together with the full chord and the drone. Send it to a large reverb with a long decay and let the tail ring into a few bars of near silence. That gap is what makes the hit feel big.

Step 7: Mix and export

  1. Keep the drone below everything else.
  2. Use a high-pass filter on the strings and motif so they don't fight the drone.
  3. Send strings and motif to a large reverb, but check the sends don't pile up into mush.
  4. Leave dynamic range alone; heavy compression flattens the build.

Then export to WAV or MP3. See how to export your song. Your project stays in your browser; Tracks never uploads it.

Frequently asked questions

Can I make a cinematic score without an orchestra library?

Yes. A strings or choir SoundFont plus a synth pad covers most of what a short cue needs. You can also load your own .sf2 file if you have a larger library.

What BPM is best for a cinematic cue?

60 to 90 BPM is typical. Many cues feel slower than their BPM because the chords change every one or two bars.

How do I make the hit feel huge?

Silence just before it, a layered low drum, the full chord, and a long reverb tail afterward. Contrast does most of the work.

Do I need music theory?

Not much. Stay in one minor key, use whole-note chords, and repeat a short motif. If you are stuck on notes, try making a melody without theory.
