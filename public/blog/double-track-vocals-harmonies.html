How to double-track vocals and stack harmonies in your browser

By Tongkit Leong · October 4, 2026 · 6 min read

Double-tracking means recording the same vocal line twice and playing both takes together; harmonies are extra lines sung at other pitches. In the Tracks browser studio you record each pass on its own track, pan the pair, and tuck them under the lead to get a wide, full chorus.

What double-tracking and harmonies actually do

A single vocal take sounds clear and intimate, but it can also sound thin, especially in a chorus. Two ways to fix that are doubling and harmony.

A double is the same line sung a second time. Because no human repeats a performance exactly, the two takes drift slightly in timing and pitch, and your ear hears one bigger, richer voice. A harmony is a different line sung at a different pitch, usually a third above or a fifth below the melody, so the vocal becomes a chord instead of a single note.

Step 1: get the lead right first

Everything else copies the lead, so spend your time there. Record a few passes, then use take comping to pick the best phrase from each pass and build one finished lead. If you have not recorded vocals in the browser before, start with how to record vocals in your browser and take comping.

Use a count-in and a metronome so every later pass lines up with the grid. Headphones are essential here: if the lead leaks from your speakers into the mic, you will record it into every double.

Step 2: record the double on its own track

Add a new audio track, arm it, and sing the line again while listening to the finished lead in your headphones. Keep each pass on its own track instead of recording over the lead, so you can balance and pan them separately later.

Match the performance, not the pitch grid

Aim to copy the lead's phrasing: where you breathe, where words start and end, and how you pronounce the sharp consonants such as "t" and "s". Do not try to be identical. The small differences are exactly what makes a double sound like a double rather than a copy.

Sing it a little softer

A double usually works best as a supporting layer. Singing it with slightly less force keeps it from competing with the lead once you blend them.

Do not copy and paste the lead

Duplicating the same audio onto a second track and nudging it only makes the vocal louder and phasey. It does not create the natural variation a real second take gives you.

Step 3: pan the pair

There are two common layouts, and both are easy to set with each track's pan control:

  • Lead center, doubles split: keep the lead in the middle and pan one double left and one right. The lead stays solid and focused while the doubles widen it.
  • Lead and one double, lightly panned: pan each a small amount to opposite sides for a subtle widening that holds up on small speakers.

For more on where to place things in the stereo field, see panning and stereo width explained.

Step 4: add harmony parts

Harmonies are extra lines on their own tracks. If you are unsure which notes to sing, a few reliable starting points:

  • A third above the melody: the most common, sweet-sounding harmony. Stay within the song's key so the notes fit the chords.
  • A fifth below: a more open, supportive sound that sits underneath the lead.
  • An octave above or below: not a true harmony, but a quick way to thicken a chorus without any note-choice risk.

Write the harmony notes on a piano roll with a simple instrument first and play them back with the chord progression, then sing along to check they work. The Tracks piano roll is a good place to sketch this, and chord progressions in the piano roll covers how to lay out the chords underneath.

If your harmony is slightly out of tune, the built-in autotune can tighten pitch. Use it gently so the stack still sounds like people, not a machine.

Step 5: balance, EQ, and glue

With several vocal tracks running, three habits keep the result clean:

  • Set the layers below the lead. Pull doubles and harmonies down until you feel them more than you hear them. If you can pick out each one individually, they are too loud.
  • High-pass the supporting tracks. Use the per-track EQ to roll off the low end on doubles and harmonies so the stack does not build up mud under the lead.
  • Route them to a group bus. Send all the supporting vocals to one group, add a compressor there, and use one shared reverb send so they sit in the same space. Group buses and sends shows how.

Step 6: check the result in mono

Wide vocals can lose strength when a phone speaker or a mono system adds the left and right sides together. Make sure the lead stays centered and that the vocal still sounds full if the stereo image narrows. If a double seems to vanish, pan it less or re-record that line.

Common mistakes

  • Doubling everything: doubling every verse removes the lift the chorus should have. Save the full stack for the sections that need to feel big.
  • Sloppy timing: if a double starts words noticeably earlier or later than the lead, it sounds like an echo rather than a thicker voice. Re-record or trim the clip start.
  • Too many parts: two doubles and one or two harmonies are plenty for most songs. More layers usually add mud, not size.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a double and a harmony?

A double repeats the same notes as the lead to thicken it. A harmony sings different notes at the same time to build a chord.

How many vocal layers do I need?

A lead with one or two doubles is enough for a thick chorus. Add one or two harmony parts only where the song needs more lift.

Can I record all of this in the browser?

Yes. Tracks records your microphone straight into audio tracks, and it includes per-track EQ and pan, group buses, sends, and autotune. Open the studio, allow microphone access, and record each pass on its own track. Your recordings stay on your device and are not uploaded.

Do I need a good microphone?

A decent mic helps, but consistency matters more. Use the same mic, distance, and room for every pass so the layers blend instead of sounding like different singers.
