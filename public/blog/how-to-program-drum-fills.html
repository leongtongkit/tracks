How to program drum fills and transitions in your browser

By Tongkit Leong · October 5, 2026 · 6 min read

A drum fill is a short change in the drum pattern, usually in the last beat or bar of a phrase, that signals the next section is coming. To program one, clear the hi-hats in the last beat, add a snare or tom roll that gets louder as it goes, and land the kick on beat 1 of the next bar. You can do all of it in the piano roll of the free Tracks studio.

Why fills matter

A loop that repeats unchanged for three minutes feels flat, even when every sound is good. A fill is a small break in the pattern that tells the listener something is about to change: a verse is ending, a chorus is arriving, or a drop is coming. It does not need to be complicated. A single extra snare hit can do the job.

Step 1: Decide where the fill goes

Fills sit at the end of a phrase. Most songs group bars in fours or eights, so the natural spots are the last beat or two of bar 4 or bar 8, right before the new section begins. If you are not sure how your song is laid out, see song structure and arrangement first.

Do not put a fill at the end of every phrase. Fills work because they are a change, so use one every four or eight bars at most, and skip some phrase ends entirely.

Step 2: Make room in the pattern

Open your drum clip in the piano roll. If it is a one-bar loop, extend it to four bars so the fill has its own bar to live in. Then go to the last beat or two of the final bar and delete the steady hi-hat hits there. A fill is easier to hear when the hats stop, because the new hits are not competing with a busy pattern.

Step 3: Place the roll

Now add snare or tom hits in the space you cleared. Three patterns cover most situations:

  • Simple: one extra snare on the "and" of beat 4, just before the downbeat.
  • Roll: snare hits on every step of the finest grid across the last beat, so they run together into a quick roll.
  • Tom run: a snare hit, then tom hits spread across the last two beats, for a more melodic fill.

Keep the kick and the main snare backbeat steady for the earlier part of the bar so the groove does not fall apart. Only the last beat or two should change.

Step 4: Build the velocity

This is the step that makes a fill sound played, not pasted in. A roll where every hit has the same velocity sounds like a machine gun. Instead, start the roll soft and raise the velocity of each hit toward the end, so the roll swells into the next section. You adjust velocity with the bars below the notes in the piano roll; velocity editing in the piano roll covers the details.

You can also run the other way. A roll that starts loud and fades, or one that has a deliberate accent on one hit, can sound looser and more human. Try both and listen.

Step 5: Land the downbeat

A fill has to resolve. On beat 1 of the next bar, place the kick, and consider adding a clap or an open hi-hat for emphasis. The first hit of a new section should be the loudest thing in the bar.

One trick that costs nothing: leave a very short gap just before the downbeat by removing the last hit of the fill. That tiny silence makes the downbeat land harder. The same idea is used on a bigger scale in a buildup and drop.

Step 6: Humanize it

If the roll still feels stiff, nudge a few hits slightly off the grid or use the humanize function to vary timing and velocity a little. Keep it subtle. For more on this, see humanize MIDI explained.

Common mistakes

  • Fills that are too busy. A fill that fills the whole bar steals attention from the song. Keep it to the last beat or two.
  • Flat velocity. Identical hit strengths make the roll sound mechanical.
  • No landing. If nothing hits on beat 1 of the next bar, the fill leads nowhere.
  • A fill every bar. Overuse turns the fill into the pattern and removes its effect.

Frequently asked questions

What is a drum fill?

A drum fill is a short variation in the drum pattern, usually in the final beat or bar of a phrase, that leads into the next section of a song.

How long should a drum fill be?

Usually one beat to one bar. Most fills in pop, hip-hop, and electronic music are a beat or two at the end of a 4- or 8-bar phrase.

Which drums should I use for a fill?

Snare and toms are the usual choices. Claps also work, especially in electronic styles. The 808 and 909 kits in Tracks include all of these; see 808 vs 909 for how they sound different.
