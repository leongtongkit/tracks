How to record clean vocals in an untreated room

By Tongkit Leong · October 18, 2026 · 7 min read

You can record clean vocals in an untreated room by getting close to the mic, surrounding yourself with soft things like blankets and clothes, and keeping the room quiet. Echo and noise are far easier to prevent at the mic than to remove afterward in Tracks.

Why untreated rooms sound bad

A bare room bounces your voice off the walls, floor, and ceiling. The mic hears your voice and a slightly delayed copy of it, which sounds hollow, boxy, or like you recorded in a bathroom. Software can reduce some of this, but it cannot cleanly separate your voice from its own reflections. The fix is to record less room in the first place.

Step 1: choose the right room

  • Smaller and softer wins. A bedroom with a bed, rug, and curtains beats a kitchen or hallway every time.
  • Avoid parallel bare walls. They create the metallic ringing you hear when you clap.
  • Clap test. Clap once. If you hear a ring or flutter, add more soft material. If the clap dies quickly, you are good.
  • A closet full of clothes is a classic vocal booth for a reason: hanging clothes absorb a lot of sound.

Step 2: build a cheap shield

You do not need foam panels. Heavy blankets, duvets, pillows, and a mattress or couch cushion all absorb sound.

  • Hang a blanket behind you and one on each side if you can.
  • Sing toward soft material, not toward a window, mirror, or bare wall. Reflections from the wall in front of your mouth matter most.
  • Put a rug or blanket on a hard floor under the mic.
  • Keep the mic away from room corners, which boost low frequencies.

Step 3: remove noise at the source

Turn off the fan, air conditioner, and anything humming. Close windows, silence phones, and avoid recording when the neighbors are loud. A computer fan close to the mic shows up in every take, so move the mic away from the machine if you can.

Step 4: distance and gain

Closer mic, less room. Sit about a hand-width or two from the mic and stay at a consistent distance. A pop filter, or a little angle off-axis, helps with plosive sounds like "p" and "b".

Then set input gain so your loudest line peaks comfortably below clipping. A clipped take cannot be fixed, while a slightly quiet one can be turned up. See gain staging to avoid clipping for how to set levels.

Step 5: record in Tracks

  1. Open the studio and allow microphone access when your browser asks.
  2. Arm a new audio track and pick your mic as the input.
  3. Record a few seconds of silence first. This is your room tone, and it tells you how much noise you need to remove.
  4. Use the count-in so you start in time, then record several takes.
  5. Wear headphones so the backing track does not leak into the mic.

Your recordings stay in your browser on your own device. Back up the project file when you finish a session; see how to back up your project.

Step 6: clean up what is left

Gate the gaps

A noise gate closes between phrases so room hiss and breathing drop out. Set the threshold just above your room tone and use gentle release so word endings do not get chopped. The full walkthrough is in noise gate for clean recordings.

High-pass the rumble

Add a high-pass filter in the track EQ at roughly 80 to 120 Hz to remove traffic, desk bumps, and low room buildup. Vocals do not need that range.

Be careful with reverb

Room echo baked into a take cannot be removed, and adding reverb on top makes it worse. Keep the recording dry and add a reverb send afterward, where you control the amount. See reverb and delay explained.

Common mistakes

  • Recording far from the mic to avoid pops, which just lets in more room.
  • Singing at a window or mirror. Flat glass reflects sound straight back.
  • Setting gain too hot. Distortion from clipping is permanent.
  • Skipping room tone. You then guess at the gate threshold.
  • Judging on laptop speakers. Check the take on headphones.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need acoustic foam panels?

No. Thin foam mostly absorbs high frequencies and does little for the boxy low-mid sound of a small room. Heavy blankets, clothes, and furniture do more for far less money.

Can software remove echo after recording?

Not cleanly. Tools can reduce noise and some ringing, but echo is part of the same signal as your voice. Prevention at the mic is the reliable fix.

Does it matter which mic I use?

Less than room and distance. A headset or laptop mic in a soft, quiet room often beats an expensive mic in an echoey kitchen. Tracks records from whatever input your browser can see. For the basics of capturing a voice, read how to record vocals in your browser.

What should I do after recording?

Pick the best parts with take comping, then follow the vocal chain order for EQ, compression, and de-essing.
