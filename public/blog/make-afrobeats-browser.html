How to make afrobeats music in your browser

By Tongkit Leong · August 7, 2026 · 8 min read

Afrobeats runs on a syncopated log drum bassline, a rolling shaker groove, and a laid-back kick pattern that sits just behind the beat, all held together by call-and-response vocal chops — and you can program every layer from scratch in the Tracks browser studio with no software to install and no account to create.

The anatomy of an afrobeats groove

Afrobeats draws on highlife, dancehall, and West African percussion traditions, and a handful of recurring elements set it apart from other genres you might already know:

  • Log drum bassline — a short, percussive, pitched tone that slides and dips between two or three notes, acting as both bass and rhythm instrument at once.
  • Rolling shaker groove — a continuous, lightly swung 16th-note pattern that gives the beat its constant forward motion, closer to a shaker or hi-hat roll than a stop-start rhythm.
  • Laid-back kick and clap — the kick sits slightly behind the grid rather than locking rigidly to it, with a clap or rimshot anchoring beat 3.
  • Call-and-response vocals — a lead vocal phrase answered by a short chopped response, a structure borrowed directly from West African vocal tradition.
  • Steady, groove-forward arrangement — afrobeats tends to keep the core groove running throughout rather than stripping it back to nothing, building energy by layering instead of dropping out.

Tracks covers all of it out of the box — the 808/909 drum machine for the kick and shaker, a subtractive synth for the log drum, the sampler for vocal chops, and a mixer to glue the whole groove together.

Step 1: Set your tempo

Open the Tracks studio and set the BPM in the transport bar.

  • Typical range: 100–115 BPM, noticeably slower and more relaxed than trap or house.
  • 106–108 BPM is a solid, genre-typical default if you're starting from scratch.
  • The groove should feel unhurried even when the shaker pattern underneath is busy — that contrast is part of the afrobeats pocket.

Step 2: Program the laid-back kick and clap

Add a track and open the drum machine. On a 16-step grid representing one bar, place kicks on beats 1 and 3, but nudge them a touch behind the grid line if your version of the sequencer allows manual timing offsets — the laid-back feel comes from the kick landing just a hair late rather than snapping precisely to the beat. Add a clap or rimshot on beat 3, layered with or just after the kick.

Resist the urge to fill the kick pattern out further. Afrobeats kicks are sparse compared to house or trap — the groove's energy comes from the shaker and log drum, not from a busy kick pattern.

Step 3: Build a rolling shaker groove

This is what gives an afrobeats beat its constant motion. Fill in a shaker or closed hi-hat sound across straight 16th notes for the full bar, then apply a light swing — see Quantize and swing explained for how swing works in the Tracks piano roll and drum machine — so the pattern rolls instead of ticking mechanically.

Taper the velocity slightly across each group of four steps so some hits sit forward and others recede, which reads as a live, hand-played shaker rather than a static loop. Keep this layer running continuously through most of the arrangement; unlike a hi-hat roll that builds and releases, the afrobeats shaker groove is meant to feel steady and hypnotic.

Step 4: Write a log drum bassline

Add a new instrument track and open the subtractive synth. Set the oscillator to a short, percussive tone — a sine or triangle wave with a fast filter envelope that snaps the attack and decays quickly — so each note reads as a pitched drum hit rather than a sustained bass note.

Open the piano roll and write a syncopated pattern that dips between two or three notes a few semitones apart, landing in the gaps left by the kick rather than doubling it. The log drum is doing double duty here as both the bassline and a percussive rhythm element, so let it move more than a typical bassline would — short glides between adjacent notes give it the sliding, vocal-like quality that defines the sound.

Locking the log drum to the kick

Loop the kick and log drum together and listen for collisions. If the log drum lands directly on top of a kick hit, offset it by a sixteenth note so the two read as a single interlocking pattern instead of competing low-end hits — the same principle covered in How to write a bassline that locks with the kick drum.

Step 5: Layer call-and-response vocal chops

Record a short vocal phrase through your microphone, or import a vocal one-shot, then use the Tracks 16-pad sampler to chop it into individual hits — see How to chop and flip a sample into a beat for the full chopping workflow.

Arrange a lead phrase on the downbeat, then trigger a shorter chopped response — a single word, an ad-lib, or a repeated syllable — in the gap that follows. Alternate between the two across the groove. This call-and-response pattern is one of the clearest fingerprints of afrobeats and works even with a handful of short, simple chops.

Step 6: Arrange with a steady groove

Afrobeats arrangements build by adding layers on top of a groove that keeps running, rather than stripping everything back between sections. A workable structure:

  1. Intro (4–8 bars): shaker and log drum alone, establishing the groove before anything else enters.
  2. Verse (8–16 bars): kick and clap enter, lead vocal phrase comes in over the groove.
  3. Pre-hook (4 bars): call-and-response vocal chops intensify, maybe a short percussion fill.
  4. Hook (8–16 bars): every layer running together — kick, shaker, log drum, and full vocal call-and-response.
  5. Breakdown (4–8 bars): drop the kick briefly, keep the shaker and log drum groove running underneath before building back in.

Use the Tracks timeline to duplicate sections and mute individual tracks so you can compare how the groove feels with and without each layer before committing to the final arrangement.

Step 7: Mix and export

Before exporting, balance levels in the Tracks mixer:

  1. Compress the log drum with a fast attack and moderate ratio so its percussive snap stays consistent against the kick.
  2. If the log drum and kick clash in the low end, add a light sidechain duck on the log drum triggered by the kick — see Sidechain compression explained for how to set it up in Tracks.
  3. Keep the shaker pattern from washing out the top end with a touch of high-shelf EQ cut if it feels harsh at full volume.
  4. Group the kick, shaker, and log drum onto a drum bus and glue them together with light bus compression so the groove reads as one unit — see How to use group buses and sends to organize a mix.

Once the balance feels right, export to WAV for a clean master file or MP3 to share online — see How to export your song to MP3 or WAV for the full walkthrough. Your project stays in your browser the whole time; Tracks never uploads it anywhere.

Frequently asked questions

What BPM is afrobeats music?

Most afrobeats sits between 100 and 115 BPM, noticeably slower than genres like house or trap. The relaxed tempo is part of what gives the genre its laid-back, groove-forward feel even when the shaker pattern underneath is busy.

What is a log drum in afrobeats production?

The log drum is a short, percussive, pitched instrument sound that carries both the bassline and a rhythmic role at once, sliding between a small handful of notes. In Tracks you can build one with the subtractive synth using a fast filter envelope and a short decay so each note snaps like a drum hit rather than sustaining like a bass note.

Do I need real Afrobeat samples or drum kits?

No. Tracks' built-in 808 and 909 drum machines cover the kick and clap sounds, the subtractive synth builds a convincing log drum tone, and the sampler handles shaker loops or vocal chops you record or import yourself. Afrobeats is defined far more by its rhythm and groove than by any specific sample pack.

How is afrobeats different from Amapiano or dancehall?

Afrobeats, Amapiano, and dancehall share West African and Caribbean roots and often overlap in tempo, but afrobeats leans on the log drum bassline and call-and-response vocals described here, while Amapiano is built around deep, rolling bass synths and jazzy piano chords, and dancehall centers on a distinct riddim-style drum pattern. The production techniques in this guide — the sampler, subtractive synth, and drum machine in Tracks — apply to all three; only the specific patterns and tones change.
