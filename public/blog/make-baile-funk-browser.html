How to make baile funk in your browser

By Tongkit Leong · August 24, 2026 · 8 min read

Baile funk (funk carioca) is built from the tamborzão rhythm, a punchy distorted 808 kick pattern, and chopped vocal shouts thrown back and forth in call-and-response — and you can put all three together from scratch in the Tracks browser studio in an afternoon, with no software to install and no account to create.

The anatomy of a baile funk track

Baile funk grew out of Rio de Janeiro's favela sound-system parties, and the whole genre is built for that setting: loud speakers, a dancing crowd, and a rhythm everyone in the room already knows. A handful of elements show up in almost every baile funk track:

  • The tamborzão — a two-bar percussion pattern of layered kick and tom-style hits that functions as the genre's signature groove, closer to a drum riff than a standard beat.
  • A punchy, sparse 808 kick — a distorted sub-heavy kick placed for maximum impact rather than density, built to slam on a sound system.
  • Chopped vocal shouts and hooks — short vocal one-shots, ad-libs, and call-and-response phrases that repeat and answer each other across the beat.
  • A minimal, repetitive arrangement — short looping sections built around one hook, with almost no chord progression or melody underneath.
  • Loud, direct mixing — little reverb, tight low end, and vocals pushed forward so the track reads clearly through a phone speaker or a street sound system alike.

Tracks covers this end to end — the drum machine for the tamborzão and kick pattern, the 16-pad sampler for chopping and triggering vocal shouts, and a mixer with EQ and compression to keep the whole thing loud and direct.

Step 1: Set your tempo

Open the Tracks studio and set the BPM in the transport bar. Baile funk sits in a fairly narrow window:

  • Most baile funk: 125–130 BPM. The pocket where the tamborzão feels natural and danceable.
  • Faster, more aggressive styles: up to 135 BPM.

130 BPM is a safe default — it's the tempo most classic tamborzão patterns were built around, and it leaves room to push faster once the groove is locked in.

Step 2: Program the tamborzão rhythm

Add a track and open the Tracks drum machine. The tamborzão is a two-bar pattern rather than a simple one-bar loop, so program it across a full two bars before you repeat it — a short first bar that sets up tension and a second bar that answers it is the classic shape.

Build it from layered percussion hits rather than a standard kick-and-snare pattern: a low tom-style hit for the body, a mid hit for movement, and the occasional rim or clap accent. Space the hits so there's real silence between them — the tamborzão gets its identity from its rhythm and gaps, not from density. Loop the two-bar pattern and nudge individual hits earlier or later until it locks into a groove that feels right against a four-on-the-floor pulse. See Quantize and swing explained if you want the pattern to sit slightly off-grid rather than perfectly mechanical.

Keep it a riff, not a beat

The easiest mistake is treating the tamborzão like a generic drum pattern that just needs a kick and a snare swapped in. Think of it instead as a short rhythmic riff, the same way you'd write a bassline or a synth hook — it should be recognizable and singable on its own, because in a real baile funk track it usually plays for the entire song with almost nothing else changing underneath it.

Step 3: Build a punchy 808 kick pattern

Layer a second, deeper kick under the tamborzão using the drum machine's 808-style kick or the subtractive synth tuned low with a short pitch envelope for punch. Keep this kick pattern sparse — often just one or two hits per bar landing in the gaps the tamborzão leaves open, so each hit reads as a distinct impact instead of blurring into a wall of low end.

Push a touch of distortion or saturation onto the kick channel so it holds up on small speakers as well as a loud system — see Chorus, phaser, distortion, and bitcrusher explained for how the distortion module works in Tracks. If the tamborzão's low tom and the 808 kick start to clash, notch a little space out of one with EQ rather than turning either one down.

Step 4: Chop and place vocal shouts

Baile funk's other defining ingredient is its vocals — short shouted hooks, ad-libs, and call-and-response phrases rather than a sung verse-chorus structure. Record your own shouts through a mic, or bring in an a cappella file, and load it into the Tracks 16-pad sampler.

Chop the recording into short one-shots: individual words, a full hook phrase, and a few short ad-libs. Assign each chop to its own pad, then trigger them rhythmically against the tamborzão and kick pattern so the vocal answers the drums instead of just riding on top of them, the call-and-response feel that defines the genre. See How to chop and flip a sample for the full sampler workflow, and pitch a chop up or down slightly between placements to keep repeated hooks from feeling static.

Step 5: Arrange around the hook

Baile funk arrangements are deliberately minimal — the goal is repetition you can dance to, not a long build with distinct sections. A workable shape looks like this:

  1. Intro (4–8 bars): tamborzão alone, or with a single vocal shout to set the hook.
  2. First drop (16–32 bars): full tamborzão, 808 kick, and the main vocal hook locked in on loop.
  3. Breakdown (8–16 bars): thin the drums out, keep vocal ad-libs going, to give the ear a short break from the full pattern.
  4. Second drop (16–32 bars): the full pattern returns, often with a second hook or vocal variation layered in.
  5. Outro (4–8 bars): strip back to the tamborzão or a single vocal chop and end abruptly rather than fading out.

Block these sections out on the Tracks timeline before polishing any individual part — see How to arrange a song for more on building a structure from loops, and How to make reggaeton in your browser for another rhythm-first, riddim-based genre built the same way.

Step 6: Mix and export

Baile funk is meant to be loud and direct, so mix with that in mind:

  1. Balance the tamborzão and 808 kick first, since together they're the whole low end of the track — check the mix at a lower volume too, since baile funk is often played on small phone or laptop speakers as well as big sound systems.
  2. Push the vocal chops forward in the mix rather than blending them into the beat; they're the hook, and they should read clearly even over a busy drum pattern.
  3. Use compression lightly on the kick and vocal channels to keep both consistent and punchy without squashing the dynamics that make the hits land.
  4. Keep reverb and delay minimal — baile funk favors a dry, in-your-face sound over spacious mixing.

When the balance feels right, export to WAV for a clean master file or MP3 to share online — see How to export your song to MP3 or WAV for the full walkthrough. Your project stays in your browser the entire time; Tracks never uploads it anywhere.

Frequently asked questions

What BPM is baile funk?

Most baile funk sits between 125 and 130 BPM, with faster, more aggressive styles pushing up to 135 BPM. 130 BPM is a reliable starting point.

What is the tamborzão?

The tamborzão is the signature two-bar percussion pattern behind most baile funk tracks, built from layered kick and tom-style hits with deliberate space between them. It functions more like a rhythmic riff than a generic drum beat, and it's usually what listeners recognize the genre by before anything else plays.

Do I need real vocal samples to make baile funk?

No — you can record your own shouts and ad-libs through a mic directly into Tracks and chop them in the sampler. What matters is the call-and-response placement against the drum pattern, not where the vocal came from.

Why does my baile funk beat feel too busy or too empty?

The tamborzão relies on gaps as much as hits, so if a pattern feels too busy, try removing hits instead of adding variation. If it feels too empty, check whether the 808 kick and vocal chops are actually filling those gaps in call-and-response — the genre's energy comes from that interplay, not from constant sound.
