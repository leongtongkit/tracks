How to mix vocals over a beat so they sit in the track

By Tongkit Leong · October 10, 2026 · 7 min read

A vocal sits in a beat when it is louder than the drums, cleaned up with EQ, evened out with compression, and placed with short reverb and delay sends. Do those steps in that order in Tracks and a raw recording stops sounding pasted on top of the instrumental.

Why vocals feel pasted on top of a beat

A beat is already a finished stack of drums, bass, and samples. A vocal recorded in a quiet room is dry, close, and full of its own low rumble, so it sounds like it belongs to a different place than the instrumental. Mixing is the job of making both feel like they were played in the same room at the same time.

The fix is rarely one magic plugin. It is a short chain, applied in a sensible order, with the vocal always judged against the beat playing underneath it.

Step 1: Set the level before any effects

Load the beat and your vocal on separate tracks, loop the verse, and set the vocal fader so you can understand every word without straining. Do this first, because effects change how loud something feels and you will chase your tail if the base level is wrong. If you have not recorded yet, how to record vocals in your browser covers a clean take.

Step 2: Clean the low end with EQ

Open the track EQ and do the subtractive moves first:

  • High-pass the rumble. Roll off everything below roughly 80 Hz. Mic stand bumps, room hum, and breath thumps live down there, and the kick and bass of the beat need that space.
  • Cut boxy low mids. If the voice sounds like it is inside a cardboard box, a small cut somewhere around 250 to 400 Hz opens it up.
  • Add air last. A gentle lift above about 8 to 10 kHz keeps words clear over hi-hats. Go small; a little goes a long way.

These are starting points. Sweep the band while the beat plays and stop where the voice gets clearer, not just brighter. The EQ cheat sheet by instrument lists the usual ranges.

Step 3: Compress so every word stays audible

Rap and sung vocals both swing between whispers and shouts. Add the compressor and lower the threshold until the loudest words trigger a few dB of gain reduction. A moderate ratio, a fast-ish attack, and a release that lets the compressor recover between phrases is a sensible start. You are aiming for a vocal that stays in front without anyone noticing the compressor. For the controls in detail, see compressor vs limiter explained.

Step 4: Tame the S sounds

Compression and a high-end lift both make sibilance (harsh S and T sounds) louder. Add the de-esser after them and reduce the band until the hiss stops cutting through. See the de-esser guide for how to find the right frequency.

Step 5: Place it with reverb and delay sends

Use the send controls rather than putting reverb directly on the track. Send a small amount of the vocal to a short reverb, which gives it a sense of space, and a second send to a tempo-synced delay for echoes that land on the beat. Keep both low enough that you only notice them when you mute them. If the vocal gets washed out, shorten the reverb or cut the send level. More on this in reverb and delay explained.

Step 6: Check against a reference and in mono

Drop a finished song in your genre onto a spare track and flip between it and your mix at the same loudness. Ask whether your vocal is louder, darker, or more distant than theirs. Then fold your mix to mono to confirm the vocal stays centered and clear. The reference track guide shows the method.

Common mistakes

  • Judging the vocal solo. A vocal that sounds great alone can disappear in the full mix. Always adjust with the beat playing.
  • Too much reverb. It pushes the voice back and blurs the lyrics. Keep the lead drier than the backing parts.
  • Skipping the high-pass. Low rumble adds up and muddies the kick and bass.
  • Fighting a loud beat. If the instrumental is already mastered very loud, lower its fader a little instead of pushing the vocal until it clips. See gain staging.

Frequently asked questions

What order should vocal effects go in?

EQ to clean, compression to even out, de-esser to control harshness, then reverb and delay on sends. The vocal chain order guide explains why.

How loud should the vocal be compared to the beat?

Loud enough that the words are clear on small speakers without straining, and not so loud that it floats above the music. Check on headphones and a phone speaker.
