How to make Jersey club in your browser

By Tongkit Leong · August 11, 2026 · 8 min read

Jersey club is built from a triplet-heavy kick pattern, chopped vocal loops, the genre's signature bed-squeak sample, and a sparse low bassline — and you can program all of it from scratch in the Tracks browser studio in an afternoon, with no software to install and no account to create.

The anatomy of a Jersey club track

Jersey club grew out of Newark house and ballroom music, and its identity comes from rhythm and sampling rather than melody or chord changes. A few traits separate it from other club genres:

  • Triplet kick groupings — instead of a straight four-on-the-floor pulse, kicks arrive in fast groups of three, giving the groove its distinctive skip.
  • Chopped vocal hooks — a single vocal phrase or acapella cut into short one- or two-word chops and repeated as the track's main hook.
  • The bed-squeak — a short, high-pitched squeak sample (originally sourced from a bed spring) that lands on off-beat accents as a rhythmic ear-catcher.
  • A sparse, low bassline — kept simple and out of the kick's way, more about weight than melody.
  • Short, loop-driven arrangement — built in 8- or 16-bar sections meant for continuous DJ mixing rather than a verse-chorus song structure.

Tracks has everything this needs — the drum machine for the triplet kick and bed-squeak, the 16-pad sampler for chopping vocal loops, a subtractive synth and piano roll for the bassline, and a timeline built around short looping sections.

Step 1: Set your tempo

Open the Tracks studio and set the BPM in the transport bar. Jersey club sits in a fairly narrow tempo band:

  • 130–133 BPM: a slightly laid-back pocket, common in remix-style Jersey club edits.
  • 134–137 BPM: the most common range, and where 135 BPM works as a dependable starting point.
  • 138–140 BPM: a faster, more energetic feel used for peak-time club edits.

Pick a tempo before writing anything else — the triplet kick pattern only feels right at Jersey club speed, and the same pattern dragged to a slower tempo just sounds like a stumble rather than a bounce.

Step 2: Program the triplet kick pattern

Add a track and open the drum machine. On a 16-step grid, the classic Jersey club move is to replace some of the straight quarter-note kicks with a fast triplet group — three quick kicks packed into the space of a beat. A common pattern:

  • A single kick on step 1.
  • A triplet group of three kicks clustered right before step 5, landing just ahead of the beat.
  • A single kick on step 9.
  • Another triplet group clustered before step 13.

Pick a short, punchy kick sample with a fast decay — a long, boomy kick will smear the triplet hits into mush. Loop the pattern alone first and listen for the skip-step bounce; if the triplet groups blur together, shorten the kick's decay a little further until each of the three hits stays distinct.

Step 3: Chop a vocal sample into a loop

Vocal chops are the melodic center of most Jersey club tracks. Load a vocal one-shot or acapella clip into the 16-pad sampler, then slice it into short segments — single words or even syllables work best. Assign each chop to its own pad.

Lay the chops onto the drum grid to build a repeating call-and-response phrase, leaving gaps between chops rather than stacking them back to back. A phrase that repeats every one or two bars, with small variations every fourth or eighth bar, is the standard approach — see How to chop and flip a sample into a beat for the full chopping workflow in Tracks' sampler.

Keeping the chops rhythmic

Nudge each chop slightly off the strict grid if it feels stiff — Jersey club vocal hooks often sit a hair ahead of or behind the beat rather than locked dead-on, which is part of what gives the genre its bounce.

Step 4: Add the bed-squeak and low percussion

The bed-squeak is one of Jersey club's most recognizable signatures — a short, high, squeaking sound that originated from sampling a bed spring and has stuck around as a genre staple. If you don't have a bed-squeak sample handy, a short pitched-up synth blip or a squeaky one-shot from any percussion pack gets close enough.

Place the squeak on off-beat accents — steps 4, 8, 12, or 14 work well — so it punctuates the triplet kick groups rather than competing with them. Underneath it, add a low tom or 808 hit right on the triplet groups to give them extra low-end punch without turning them into a full second bassline.

Step 5: Build a sparse bassline

Add a synth track and open the subtractive synth. Set a rounded sawtooth or sine-based patch and pull the filter down so the tone stays low and out of the way of the vocal chops.

Open the piano roll and write a short, simple pattern — often just one or two low notes that land on the downbeats and leave space for the kick triplets to punch through cleanly. Jersey club basslines are supporting weight, not a melodic hook, so resist the urge to add extra movement; a bassline that's too busy will fight the triplet kick pattern for rhythmic attention.

Step 6: Arrange in short, looping sections

Jersey club tracks are built for continuous DJ mixing, so arrangements favor short, clearly defined loops over a traditional verse-chorus structure. A typical shape:

  1. Intro (8–16 bars): kick and bed-squeak only, giving a DJ room to mix in.
  2. Build (8 bars): bring in the vocal chops and bassline.
  3. Main loop (16–32 bars): full arrangement — triplet kicks, bassline, vocal chops, bed-squeak — repeating with small variation every 8 bars.
  4. Breakdown (8 bars): strip back to vocal chops and light percussion for contrast.
  5. Second main loop (16–32 bars): full arrangement returns, often with a new vocal chop variation.
  6. Outro (8–16 bars): back to kick and bed-squeak alone for a clean DJ mix-out.

Keep sections in multiples of 4 or 8 bars — Jersey club edits are frequently mixed back-to-back by DJs, and tight, predictable bar lengths make that far easier. See How to arrange a song: verse, chorus, bridge structure for more on blocking out sections on the Tracks timeline.

Step 7: Mix and export

Before you export, spend a few minutes balancing levels in the Tracks mixer:

  1. Balance the kick and bassline first so the triplet groups stay punchy and distinct instead of blurring into a low rumble.
  2. Keep the vocal chops upfront, dry, and slightly brighter than the rest of the mix — they carry the track's hook, so they need to cut through.
  3. Apply light compression on the drum bus to glue the kick and bed-squeak together without softening the triplet attack.
  4. If the bassline and kick are still crowding each other, try sidechain ducking on the bass track triggered by the kick — see Sidechain compression explained for how to set it up in Tracks.

When the balance feels right at a comfortable volume, export to WAV for a clean master file or MP3 to share online — see How to export your song to MP3 or WAV for the full walkthrough. Your project stays in your browser the entire time; Tracks never uploads it anywhere.

Frequently asked questions

What BPM is Jersey club?

Most Jersey club sits between 130 and 140 BPM, with 134–137 BPM the most common range. 135 BPM is a safe starting point if you're not chasing a specific edit style.

What makes a Jersey club kick pattern different from house or trap?

The kick doesn't sit on a straight quarter-note or half-time grid — it arrives in fast triplet groups of three, alternating with single hits on the downbeat. That triplet skip is the single most identifying feature of the genre, and it's what gives Jersey club its distinctive bounce.

Do I need a real bed-squeak sample?

It helps get the authentic sound, but it's not required. A short, high-pitched squeaky one-shot or a quickly pitched-up synth blip placed on off-beat accents gets close enough to the effect for most tracks. What matters more than the exact source sound is the placement — off the main kick hits, punctuating rather than competing.

Can I use a MIDI keyboard for the bassline instead of clicking it in?

Yes. Tracks supports Web MIDI, so if you have a MIDI keyboard connected over USB you can play the bassline live and record the performance directly into the piano roll. See How to use a MIDI keyboard in a browser DAW for the setup steps.
