How to make a warm synth pad in your browser

By Tongkit Leong · October 22, 2026 · 6 min read

A synth pad is a soft, sustained sound that fills the space behind a song, and you make one with a slow attack, a long release, a darkened filter and a little unison detune. In the Tracks browser studio you can build one from the built-in synth in a few minutes.

What a pad does in a song

Drums and bass give a song its pulse. A pad gives it a mood. It holds a chord under the other parts, so the track feels full without adding more notes to think about.

Pads show up in nearly every style, from ambient and synthwave to pop verses. If you want the genre angle, see making ambient music in the browser.

Step 1: Pick a starting wave

Open the synth on a new instrument track. Choose a sawtooth wave for a full, slightly bright pad, or a triangle wave for something softer and more hollow. A sine wave also works, but it sounds thin on its own.

If you are new to oscillators and filters, the synth sound design guide explains the basics.

Step 2: Slow the envelope

This is the step that makes a sound read as a pad. The envelope controls how a note changes over time:

  • Attack: set it slow, so each note swells in instead of snapping on.
  • Sustain: keep it high, so the note holds at full level while you hold the key.
  • Release: set it long, so the note fades out and overlaps the next chord.

Start with an attack of a few hundred milliseconds and adjust by ear. Too slow and the chord arrives late, too fast and it sounds like a plain organ.

Step 3: Darken the filter

A raw saw wave is buzzy. Lower the low-pass filter cutoff until the top end rolls off and the tone turns warm. Keep resonance low, because a high resonance adds a whistling edge that distracts from the other parts.

For movement, route a slow LFO to the cutoff so the tone breathes. LFO modulation explained shows how.

Step 4: Thicken with unison and detune

Unison stacks several slightly detuned copies of the oscillator, which spreads one note across the stereo field. For a pad, use a modest number of voices and a small detune. Too much detune sounds out of tune rather than wide. More detail in unison and detune explained.

Step 5: Add reverb

Send the pad to a reverb on the mixer. A long, soft reverb pushes the pad back and makes it feel large. Start with the send low and raise it until the pad sits behind the other instruments. If it gets cloudy, lower the send or cut low frequencies from the pad with the EQ. Reverb and delay explained covers the settings.

Step 6: Play long, simple chords

In the piano roll, draw chords that last a full bar or more. Three or four notes is plenty. Keep them in the middle of the keyboard, since a pad down low competes with the bass and one up high competes with the melody. If you need chord ideas, try chord progressions in the piano roll.

Let the chords overlap slightly, or move one note at a time between chords, so the changes feel smooth.

Keep the pad out of the way

  • Lower the volume: a pad is background, so it usually sits well under the lead.
  • Cut the low end: a high-pass filter in the EQ keeps it clear of kick and bass.
  • Pan or widen it: a wide pad leaves the center for vocals and drums.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a pad and a lead?

A lead plays the melody and cuts through the mix. A pad plays held chords underneath and stays in the background.

Why does my pad sound thin?

Usually it is a single oscillator with no unison or reverb. Add a few unison voices with small detune, then a reverb send.

Why does my pad sound muddy?

Too many low notes or too much reverb. Move the chord up, cut low frequencies with the EQ, and reduce the send.

Can I use a SoundFont instead?

Yes. Tracks plays SoundFont files, and many include pad presets. See SoundFont instruments explained.
