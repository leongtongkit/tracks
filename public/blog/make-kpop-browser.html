How to make K-pop music in your browser

By Tongkit Leong · September 10, 2026 · 10 min read

K-pop packs more into three and a half minutes than almost any other genre — a beat that shifts style between sections, a pre-chorus built to drop into a hard chorus, a rap or dance breakdown in the middle, and a vocal stack polished to a hard shine — and you can build all of it from scratch in the Tracks browser studio, with no software to install and no account to create.

The anatomy of a K-pop track

K-pop production is defined less by a single sound than by how aggressively it changes sound within one song. A few things separate a track that reads as K-pop from a track that reads as generic pop:

  • Genre-blending sections — a verse can sit in tropical house, a pre-chorus lean electro, and a chorus land somewhere between EDM and trap, all inside the same track.
  • A pre-chorus built as a setup, not a bridge — the arrangement thins out and rises specifically to drop into the chorus, more like an EDM build than a traditional pop lift.
  • A rap or dance breakdown — most title tracks carve out a contrasting middle-eight for a rapped verse or a percussion-only section built for a choreography break.
  • A dense, hyper-polished vocal stack — lead, adlibs, and tight harmony layers, often split across multiple vocalists, mixed forward and clean rather than left rough or airy.
  • A key change late in the song — a modulation into the final chorus is common, adding a last jolt of energy right when the track might otherwise start to feel repetitive.

Tracks has what this needs built in — a subtractive/FM synth and drum machine for jumping between beat styles, mic recording with take comping for the vocal stack, and a full mixer for a chain that can carry a dense arrangement without turning to mud.

Step 1: Set your tempo

Open the Tracks studio and set the BPM in the transport bar. K-pop title tracks vary more in tempo than most genres, but a few ranges show up repeatedly:

  • Mid-tempo, R&B-leaning: 90–105 BPM. Common for a moodier or more vocal-forward title track.
  • Upbeat dance-pop: 105–125 BPM. The most common range for a high-energy title track built around choreography.
  • EDM-adjacent: 125–135 BPM. Leans on house or electro-pop influence, with a harder, faster chorus drop.

It's also common for the feel to shift within a song even at a fixed BPM — a half-time verse against a double-time chorus hi-hat pattern is a reliable way to make one tempo feel like two different sections. If you want the tempo itself to change at a specific bar rather than just the feel, see How to change tempo mid-song with a tempo map.

Step 2: Build a genre-blending beat

Open the 808 or 909 drum machine and build the verse pattern first — something restrained, often closer to tropical house or R&B, with a simple kick-snare pattern and light hats. Then build a second, distinct pattern for the chorus: harder kicks, busier hats, maybe a half-time snare that hits like an EDM drop rather than a pop backbeat. Save both patterns and swap between them by section instead of trying to write one groove that covers the whole song.

This is the biggest structural difference from a straight pop beat — see How to make a pop song in your browser if you want a comparison, since that guide leans on one steady groove throughout rather than switching styles mid-track. For programming the individual patterns, Quantize and swing explained is worth a look if you want the verse to sit slightly looser than a tightly quantized chorus.

Step 3: Write chords that support a key change

Add an instrument track, load a synth pad or SoundFont piano, and open the piano roll. Write a simple progression for the verse and chorus — plain triads are fine, since the arrangement and vocal stack are doing most of the work that makes the song feel busy. See Chord progressions in the piano roll for building and auditioning shapes quickly.

Plan ahead for a key change on the final chorus. Write out the same progression transposed up a whole step or half step, and keep it ready as a separate section in the arrangement — you'll drop it in once the rest of the track is built. A late key change is one of the most recognizable K-pop arrangement tricks, and it costs almost nothing to set up if the chords are already written.

Step 4: Write the pre-chorus drop

The pre-chorus in K-pop behaves more like an EDM build than a traditional pop lift — its whole job is tension that resolves the instant the chorus hits. Strip the arrangement down to vocal, a thin chord bed, and a stripped drum pattern, then build across four or eight bars: layer instruments back in, ride a rising synth line, and automate a filter sweep or noise riser into the last beat before the chorus.

See How to use automation to make your mix move for setting up that filter sweep, and treat the very last beat before the chorus as a hard cut — silence for a fraction of a second, or a single sharp hit, reads as more dramatic than a smooth crossfade into the drop.

Step 5: Add a rap or dance breakdown

Most K-pop title tracks include a contrasting section past the midpoint that breaks the verse-chorus cycle. Two common approaches:

  • A rap verse: pull the arrangement back to mostly drums and bass, write or record a rapped verse over that sparser beat, and let the rhythm of the delivery carry the section instead of melody.
  • A dance breakdown: drop the melodic elements out almost entirely and let a tight, syncopated drum pattern carry the section alone — built for a choreography break rather than a vocal line.

Either way, this section should sound clearly different from the verse and chorus around it — a different drum pattern, a different density, sometimes even a different tempo feel — so it reads as a deliberate structural beat rather than a lull in the song.

Step 6: Layer the vocal chain

Plug in a mic and record the lead vocal using Tracks' built-in recording with count-in, then record harmony and adlib layers on separate tracks. Comp each part from multiple takes rather than settling for one pass — see Take comping: building the best vocal take. K-pop vocal production tends to be dense and forward rather than airy, so don't be shy about stacking two or three harmony takes under the lead on the chorus.

Build a vocal chain with EQ for presence, compression to keep every layer consistent, and a de-esser to control harsh "s" and "t" sounds across a stack of vocals — see Vocal chain order explained and De-esser explained. If any part needs pitch tightened, Tracks' built-in autotune can correct it cleanly without pushing into an obviously robotic effect — see Autotune in the browser: natural or robotic.

Step 7: Arrange the full song

A K-pop arrangement is usually more eventful than a straight pop song, packing extra sections and beat changes into a similar total runtime. A common layout:

  1. Intro (4–8 bars): a distinct hook or vocal chant, often over just the chorus beat, establishing the drop before the song proper starts.
  2. Verse (8–16 bars): the restrained beat variant, lower-energy vocal, setting up the story.
  3. Pre-chorus (4–8 bars): the build-and-drop section from step 4.
  4. Chorus (8–16 bars): the full, hardest-hitting beat variant, dense vocal stack, the loudest point of the song so far.
  5. Second verse and pre-chorus: often shortened to keep momentum toward the rap or dance break.
  6. Rap or dance breakdown (8–16 bars): the contrasting section from step 5.
  7. Final pre-chorus and chorus: often with the key change from step 3 landing right at this last chorus.
  8. Outro (4–8 bars): a return to the intro hook or a stripped-back vocal tag to close the track.

Track section lengths as you go — because K-pop moves through so many distinct ideas, it's easy for a full arrangement to run long. Trimming a verse or breakdown by a few bars usually helps more than adding new material.

Step 8: Mix and export

A dense arrangement and a stacked vocal need careful gain staging so nothing turns to mud once every section is layered together:

  1. EQ each vocal layer to carve its own space — the lead should sit clearly on top, with harmonies and adlibs pulled back and placed slightly wider — see EQ cheat sheet: what to cut and boost by instrument.
  2. Use group buses to control the vocal stack, the beat, and any synth layers as three separate blocks rather than mixing dozens of individual tracks at once — see Mix groups, buses, and sends explained.
  3. Watch gain staging closely once the chorus, key-changed final chorus, and full vocal stack are all playing together, since that's where a busy K-pop mix is most likely to clip — see Gain staging: how to avoid clipping and keep a healthy mix.
  4. Set a loud, competitive final master, since K-pop choruses are mixed to hit hard against other high-energy pop — see Mastering your track for streaming.

When the balance feels right, export to WAV for a clean master file or MP3 to share online — see How to export your song to MP3 or WAV. Your project stays in your browser the whole time; Tracks never uploads it anywhere.

Frequently asked questions

What BPM is K-pop?

Most K-pop title tracks fall between 90 and 135 BPM, with mid-tempo, R&B-leaning tracks around 90–105 BPM, upbeat dance-pop around 105–125 BPM, and EDM-adjacent tracks closer to 125–135 BPM. It's also common for the feel to shift between sections even at one fixed tempo.

Why does K-pop change styles so much within one song?

It's a deliberate arrangement choice, not an accident — a distinct verse, pre-chorus, chorus, and breakdown give a title track more structural variety in three and a half minutes than most other pop genres attempt. Building each section as its own beat pattern, rather than one groove that runs the whole song, is the most direct way to get that effect.

Do I need multiple vocalists to get the K-pop vocal sound?

No — you can build a convincing stack from one voice by recording separate lead, harmony, and adlib takes on different tracks and comping the best phrases from each. What matters more than the number of singers is density: layering two or three vocal parts under the chorus reads as a K-pop-style stack even with a single vocalist.

Where should the key change go?

Almost always right at the final chorus, after the last pre-chorus build. Writing the transposed chord progression as its own section early, before you finish the rest of the arrangement, makes it easy to drop in without reworking anything else.
