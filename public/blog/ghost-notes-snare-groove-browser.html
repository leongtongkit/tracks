How to program ghost notes on the snare for a groovier beat

By Tongkit Leong · October 24, 2026 · 6 min read

Ghost notes are very quiet snare hits placed between the main backbeats, and they are the quickest way to make a stiff drum loop feel played by a person. You can add them in the piano roll of the free Tracks studio by placing extra snare notes and turning their velocity way down.

What a ghost note is

A backbeat is the loud snare on beats 2 and 4. A ghost note is a soft snare hit somewhere else in the bar. You barely hear it as a distinct hit. What you hear is a bit of rattle and motion around the backbeat, which is the sound of funk, hip-hop, R&B, and a lot of pop drumming.

Real drummers play ghosts with a light touch, so they come out far quieter than the main hits. In a program the same idea works: same snare sound, much lower velocity.

Step 1: Build the backbeat

Open a drum track, create a clip, and open it in the piano roll. Put a kick on beat 1 and a snare on beats 2 and 4. If you have not built a beat yet, start with the drum machine guide. Add a steady hi-hat pattern from the hi-hat guide so you can hear the ghosts in context.

Step 2: Choose a sixteenth-note grid

Ghost notes live on the small steps between the beats, so switch the grid to sixteenth notes. That gives you four slots per beat: the beat itself, the "e", the "and", and the "a".

Step 3: Place the ghost notes

Add snare notes on weak steps. The spots that work most often are:

  • Just before the backbeat: the "a" of beat 1 or beat 3 leads into the snare on 2 or 4.
  • Just after the backbeat: the "e" of beat 2 or beat 4 trails off the hit.
  • The "and" of beat 3: a classic spot in funk and hip-hop grooves.

Start with two or three ghosts in a bar. More than that and the snare line gets busy.

Step 4: Turn the velocity down

This step is what makes them ghosts. Select the new notes and lower their velocity in the velocity lane under the piano roll, to roughly a quarter to a third of the backbeat level. If you can clearly pick out each ghost on its own, it is too loud. The velocity editing guide covers the editing tools.

Step 5: Thin and vary

A ghost pattern that repeats identically every bar becomes a pattern again. Delete a note here, move one there, and keep one or two bars slightly different. Then add a little swing or humanize so the ghosts do not land with machine precision; see quantize and swing and humanize MIDI. Keep the amount small.

Step 6: Check them against the kick

Loop the beat and listen. A ghost that lands right on a kick hit turns into mud, and a ghost too close to the backbeat can smear it. Remove any that fight with the kick. If the snare gets cloudy, try a slightly shorter snare sound or check layering kick and snare.

Common mistakes

  • Ghosts too loud. They should sit well below the backbeat.
  • Too many. Two or three per bar is plenty to start.
  • Identical every bar. Vary them across a four-bar loop.
  • Colliding with the kick. Move or delete the clashing note.

Frequently asked questions

What are ghost notes on a snare?

Ghost notes are quiet snare hits played between the main backbeat hits. They add texture and groove without being heard as separate accents.

What velocity should ghost notes be?

Much lower than the backbeat, often around a quarter to a third of its level. Adjust by ear until you feel them more than you hear them.

Do ghost notes work in every genre?

They are most common in funk, hip-hop, R&B, soul, and pop. Genres built on a rigid grid, like some techno and hardstyle, tend to leave them out.
