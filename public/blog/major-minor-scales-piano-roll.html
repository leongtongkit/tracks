Major vs minor scales: pick a key and write melodies that sound right

By Tongkit Leong · November 4, 2026 · 6 min read

A major scale sounds bright and settled; a minor scale sounds darker and more tense. Pick one scale, keep every melody note and chord inside its seven notes, and your music stays in key. You can draw all of it in the piano roll of the Tracks browser studio.

What a scale is

A scale is a set of seven notes out of the twelve available in an octave. Songs mostly use notes from one scale, which is why they feel like they belong together. That chosen scale is the song's key.

Every scale has a starting note, called the root. A song in C major has C as home base. A song in A minor has A as home base. The melody tends to end on the root because it feels like arriving.

The major scale pattern

A major scale follows a fixed spacing of whole steps (W, two keys apart) and half steps (H, one key apart): W W H W W W H. Start on C and follow it, and you land on the white keys only: C D E F G A B.

Because the pattern is fixed, you can start on any key and get a major scale. Starting on G gives G A B C D E F#, with one black key. The pattern is the scale, and the starting note only sets the key.

The minor scale pattern

The natural minor scale uses a different spacing: W H W W H W W. Start on A and you again get only white keys: A B C D E F G. That is why A minor is called the relative minor of C major. Both use the same seven notes with a different home note.

The practical difference is the third note. In C major it is E; in C minor it is E flat, one key lower. That single note is most of the bright versus dark feeling.

How to use a scale in the piano roll

The piano roll shows a keyboard down the left side, so you can see which rows are black and white keys. Tracks does not need to know your key. You just choose the notes.

Step 1: Pick a key and mood

Want something happy, bright or confident? Try C major. Want something moody, sad or tense? Try A minor. Both use only white keys, which makes them easy to see.

Step 2: Mark your seven rows

Before you place notes, decide which rows are allowed. For C major or A minor that is every white key. For another key, work out the pattern from the root and note which rows you will avoid. Everything else is off limits for now.

Step 3: Write a four-note melody

Open a synth or piano track and place four notes from your scale, with the root as the last note. Play it back. If a note sounds wrong, check that it is on an allowed row and move it.

Step 4: Add chords from the same notes

Build a chord by stacking every other note of the scale. In C major, C E G is the home chord, F A C is the next, and G B D is the tense one. In A minor, A C E is home. Chords made from scale notes always fit a melody from that scale. For more on this, see chord progressions in the piano roll.

Step 5: Land on the root

End phrases on the root note or on a chord note. This resolves the melody. Ending on other scale notes keeps tension, which is useful in the middle of a phrase.

Major or minor: which should you choose?

  • Major suits pop, upbeat dance, and feel-good songs.
  • Minor suits trap, drill, lo-fi, and moody electronic music.
  • Not sure? Write the same four-note idea in both and keep whichever fits your drums and tempo.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing keys between tracks. If the bass is in A minor and the melody is in C sharp minor, they clash. Keep every pitched track in the same key.
  • Ignoring the root. A bass line that never touches the root makes the key feel unclear.
  • Treating off-scale notes as errors. A single outside note can add colour. Use it deliberately and briefly, then return to the scale.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between major and minor?

The third note of the scale. Major has a higher third, which sounds bright. Minor has a lower third, which sounds darker. The rest of the pattern also differs, but the third is what you hear first.

What is a relative minor?

Every major key shares its seven notes with a minor key that starts three keys lower. C major and A minor are a pair. Switching between them mid-song keeps the same notes but changes the home note and mood.

Do I need to learn all twelve keys?

No. Learn C major and A minor first because they use only white keys. If you want to change key later, you can move your notes in one go, as described in how to transpose and change key.

Can I write a good melody without scales?

Yes. Sticking to the black keys gives a pentatonic scale that is hard to get wrong. See how to make a melody if you do not know music theory. Learning one major and one minor scale is the natural next step.
